## Introduction

Chapter 2 introduced the vocabulary a reader needs to talk about stocks: price-to-earnings ratio, earnings per share, market capitalization, dividend yield, beta. Chapter 3 introduced the vocabulary a reader needs to talk about real estate: net operating income, capitalization rate, cash-on-cash return, gross rent multiplier, vacancy rate, appreciation rate. Each vocabulary stood on its own, defined with respect to its own asset class.

Part II of this book will make those two vocabularies talk to each other. Before it can, the vocabularies need to be placed on the same page, with their terms aligned along the dimensions that actually matter for comparison: what does each metric measure, what does it share with its counterpart, and where do the two diverge in ways that will mislead any reader who assumes they map cleanly onto one another. That alignment is the sole job of this chapter.

Chapter 4 is deliberately short. It is a reference chapter rather than a narrative one, and readers should expect to return to it while reading Part II. The goal is not to say anything new about either asset class but to fix the correspondence between their terms so that subsequent chapters can use either vocabulary without pausing to define it, and so that readers who are more fluent in one asset class than the other can translate on the fly.

## Why Metrics Matter

A metric is a rule for turning messy reality into a single number. That compression is useful because it lets an investor compare two investments without rebuilding the underlying analysis from scratch each time. It is also dangerous because the compression is lossy. Two properties with the same cap rate can differ enormously in the durability of their cash flows, the quality of their leases, and the age of their roofs. Two stocks with the same price-to-earnings ratio can differ enormously in the quality of their earnings, the sustainability of their margins, and the amount of debt on their balance sheets. A metric is a starting point for judgment, not a substitute for it.

The comparison across asset classes introduces a second problem. A number that means one thing for a share of stock can mean something subtly different for a property, even when the formulas look parallel. A dividend yield of 4 percent on a utility stock and a cash-on-cash return of 4 percent on a rental house are not the same financial object. The utility's dividend is paid out of corporate earnings that have already absorbed capital expenditures, depreciation, and interest; the rental's cash-on-cash figure is paid out of net operating income after debt service but before depreciation, vacancy reserves, and capital expenditures that the owner will eventually have to fund. The headline numbers look identical. The economic substance is not.

The chapters that follow in Part II are built around these comparisons and will keep returning to the gap between headline parallels and economic substance. This chapter lays the groundwork by pairing each major metric with its counterpart and flagging the places where the correspondence breaks down.

## Valuation Metrics

Valuation metrics answer the question: how much am I paying per unit of what the asset produces? For stocks, the standard answer is the **price-to-earnings ratio** (P/E), defined as the share price divided by earnings per share. For real estate, it is the **capitalization rate** (cap rate), defined as net operating income divided by purchase price or current market value. The two metrics are close cousins. A P/E of 20 corresponds to an earnings yield of 5 percent; a cap rate of 5 percent means the property generates NOI equal to 5 percent of its value. Both are expressing price in units of annual economic output.

The family resemblance is real but has limits. P/E is calculated using net earnings after interest, taxes, and depreciation; cap rates are calculated using NOI, which sits above all three lines on a real estate income statement. A fairer comparison is between a stock's **enterprise-value-to-EBITDA** multiple and a property's cap rate, since EBITDA is the corporate analog of NOI. Chapter 5 will return to this point because it matters for any returns comparison that tries to match stocks and real estate on like-for-like terms.

Price-per-unit metrics offer a second layer of valuation. For stocks, **price-to-book** compares market price with the accounting book value of the company's equity. For real estate, **price per square foot** compares the purchase price with the physical size of the improvement. Neither metric is deep enough to drive a decision on its own, but both are useful as sanity checks against neighborhood or sector norms.

## Income Metrics

Income metrics answer the question: what is this asset producing for its owner, in cash or earnings, over a given period? For stocks, the two most common measures are **earnings per share** (EPS), the company's total net earnings divided by its diluted share count, and **dividend yield**, the annual dividend per share divided by the current share price. The first measures what the company earned on the shareholder's behalf; the second measures what the company actually paid to the shareholder in cash. For real estate, the parallel measures are **net operating income** (NOI), the property's gross rent minus operating expenses, and **cash-on-cash return**, the annual pre-tax cash flow after debt service divided by the cash the investor actually put into the deal.

The temptation is to pair EPS with NOI and dividend yield with cash-on-cash return. That pairing is roughly right but hides a structural difference. EPS is an accounting measure of what a company produced, calculated after interest expense and taxes; dividend yield is a cash measure of what the company chose to pay out. For stocks, in other words, the two numbers separate production from distribution. In real estate, NOI is the production measure and it is also a pre-financing, pre-tax number; cash-on-cash return is the distribution measure and it is a post-financing, pre-tax number. A real estate investor who uses heavy leverage can produce a cash-on-cash return well above the underlying cap rate, just as a company that pays out more than it earns can produce a dividend yield that outruns its EPS, and in both cases the arithmetic breaks down over time if the underlying production does not support the distribution.

A second income metric from the stock vocabulary is the **payout ratio**, the share of earnings distributed as dividends. Its real estate counterpart is less formal but no less important: the ratio of distributable cash flow to NOI, which reflects how much of the property's operating income survives debt service and ongoing capital needs. Both ratios are warning systems for distributions that look attractive in the short run but cannot be sustained.

## Return Metrics

Return metrics answer the question: how much did the asset make, in total, over a given period? For stocks, the convention is **total return**, which combines price appreciation with dividends reinvested. For real estate, the convention is also total return, but it combines price appreciation with rental cash flow net of operating expenses and, in most reporting conventions, net of financing costs. The two totals are conceptually identical. The components come from different places, and the split between income and appreciation differs sharply by asset class. Over long holding periods, rental cash flow has historically contributed more to real estate total return than price appreciation has, while for U.S. equities over the same periods capital appreciation has typically contributed the larger share. Chapter 5 develops this comparison in full.

A parallel pair of growth measures is also worth naming here. **Earnings growth rate** for a stock, often calculated on a trailing or forward basis against a chosen time window, measures the rate at which the company's earnings are expanding. **Appreciation rate** for a property measures the rate at which the property's market value is expanding. Both are annualized. Both can be distorted by short sample windows. And both are only half of the return story.

## Risk Metrics

Risk metrics answer the question: how much might this investment move, and why? For stocks, the two most common measures are **beta**, which captures a stock's sensitivity to movements in a broad market index (a beta of 1.0 means the stock has historically moved in line with the index, a beta of 1.5 means it has moved 50 percent more, and so on), and **standard deviation** of returns, which captures the typical magnitude of the stock's month-to-month or year-to-year return variation. Both measures use time series of publicly reported prices, which real estate does not have in the same form.

Real estate risk is measured differently, in part because transaction data are sparse and in part because the risks themselves are different in kind. **Vacancy rate**, introduced in Chapter 3, is the most commonly cited operational risk metric: every percentage point of vacancy is a percentage point subtracted from NOI. **Location risk** is a category rather than a number and covers everything from neighborhood demographic shifts to changes in local employment, zoning, or school district boundaries. **Market cycle risk** refers to the risk that the property is acquired near a cyclical peak and sold near a trough, a risk that is amplified by the illiquidity discussed in Chapter 10. These three together are the nearest real estate analog to beta and standard deviation, but the analogy is loose. A real estate investor can reduce beta and standard deviation only indirectly, by diversifying across markets or property types; a stock investor can reduce them directly, by holding an index fund. Chapter 6 examines the gap in full.

## Leverage Metrics

Leverage metrics answer the question: how much of the position is financed with borrowed money? For stocks, the relevant measure is the **margin ratio**, the share of a brokerage position financed with borrowed funds rather than equity. Federal Reserve Regulation T caps initial margin on most listed equities at 50 percent of the purchase price, meaning an investor cannot borrow more than half the cost of the position at the time of purchase.[^1] For real estate, the relevant measure is the **loan-to-value ratio** (LTV), the mortgage balance divided by the property's value. Conforming residential mortgages on owner-occupied homes routinely go to 95 or 97 percent LTV, and investment-property mortgages commonly go to 75 or 80 percent.[^2]

The numeric gap between the two regimes is one of the starkest structural differences in the book. A stock investor at the legal maximum is financing half the position with debt; a homeowner at the standard maximum is financing nineteen-twentieths of it. Chapter 11 devotes an entire chapter to leverage and its consequences. For the purposes of this glossary the point is simply that the word "leverage," applied without qualification, means very different orders of magnitude in the two asset classes, and readers should never assume that a side-by-side comparison of leverage ratios is comparing comparable things.

## How to Use These Metrics

The chapters in Part II will apply these metric pairs as follows. Chapter 5 uses the valuation and return rows to ask where money has grown faster over long holding periods and what the sources of that growth have been. Chapter 6 uses the risk row to examine volatility, drawdowns, and the distinct categories of risk that real estate carries which stocks do not. Chapter 7 uses the income rows to ask which asset class generates more reliable cash flow per dollar invested. Chapter 11 uses the leverage row to examine the arithmetic of borrowed capital and the conditions under which it amplifies returns or destroys them. Each of these chapters will open with a brief reminder of which rows of Table T4.1 it is about to use.

Readers should not expect the metrics to settle arguments on their own. The numbers in Part II will usually be close enough that the decision between asset classes turns on considerations this chapter cannot capture, including time, temperament, tax situation, and geography. What the metrics can do is force the argument onto an honest footing by making the translation between vocabularies explicit. A reader who does not know whether a cap rate is comparable to a P/E ratio cannot tell whether stocks or real estate are expensive; a reader who does know can. That translation is the only thing Chapter 4 is responsible for, and with it complete, Part I ends and the comparison begins.

## Key Takeaways

- Every major metric from Chapter 2 has a rough counterpart in Chapter 3, and vice versa, but the correspondence is approximate rather than exact, and the differences matter.
- Valuation metrics (P/E and cap rate) both express price in units of annual economic output, but P/E is net of interest, taxes, and depreciation while cap rate is not, so unadjusted side-by-side comparisons are misleading.
- Income metrics separate production from distribution in both asset classes. EPS and NOI measure what the asset produced; dividend yield and cash-on-cash return measure what the investor actually received.
- Return metrics are conceptually identical across asset classes, but the historical split between income and appreciation differs sharply, a point Chapter 5 develops in full.
- Risk metrics translate loosely. Beta and standard deviation have no direct real estate counterpart; vacancy rate, location risk, and market cycle risk cover the same ground differently.
- Leverage metrics differ by an order of magnitude. Regulation T caps equity margin at roughly 50 percent; residential mortgages routinely reach 75 to 97 percent LTV, and that gap is among the most consequential structural differences between the two asset classes.
- Table T4.1 is a reference to return to while reading Part II, not a summary to memorize.
