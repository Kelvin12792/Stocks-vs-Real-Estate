## Introduction

Every investment decision is made in the shadow of history. The investor who buys an index fund in 2026 is inheriting a century of institutional scaffolding — exchanges, regulators, tax rules, information systems — that did not exist for the investor of 1926. The same is true of the person who closes on a rental duplex this year: they are operating inside a mortgage market, a title system, and a tax code that took generations to build. Neither stocks nor real estate are static categories. Both have been reshaped, repeatedly, by war, legislation, technology, and crisis.

A history of the two asset classes is useful not because the past predicts the future, but because the past explains why the present looks the way it does. It explains why a middle-income household in the United States can open a brokerage account on a phone in four minutes and buy a fraction of a share for one dollar, while buying a single rental property typically requires tens of thousands of dollars of upfront capital, a credit application, an appraisal, and a closing process measured in weeks. It explains why the stock market is often described in terms of daily volatility while real estate is described in terms of decades-long cycles. It explains why, at times of panic, these two asset classes have sometimes moved together and sometimes moved in opposite directions.

This chapter traces the long arc of both investments in the United States from roughly the middle of the nineteenth century to the present. It examines how ordinary people gained access to each market, what the returns have actually looked like over long horizons, and which historical inflection points left the most durable marks. It closes with a detailed case study of the 2008 financial crisis, an event in which stocks and real estate were damaged by the same underlying shock but recovered on very different timelines.

The goal is not to decide which asset class "won" history. That framing mistakes variance for destiny. The goal is to give the rest of this book a common historical footing — so that when the later chapters compare returns, risk, cash flow, taxes, liquidity, leverage, effort, and barrier to entry, the reader understands the institutional and historical context that shaped each of those dimensions.

## The Stocks Perspective: How Ordinary People Gained Access to the Stock Market

For most of American history, the stock market was not a place where ordinary people invested. It was a place where ordinary people were sometimes allowed to speculate — at considerable disadvantage to the professionals already inside.

The New York Stock Exchange traces its origin to the **Buttonwood Agreement** (a two-sentence pact signed by 24 brokers under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street on May 17, 1792) in which the signatories agreed to trade only with each other and at fixed commission rates. That cartel structure — members only, fixed commissions — defined the stock market for the next 183 years. Retail investors participated through intermediaries who charged whatever the Exchange told them to charge, and price discovery was dominated by a small number of specialists and floor brokers.

Outside the Exchange, the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the rise of **bucket shops** (storefront operations where customers bet on stock price movements without actually owning any shares). Bucket shops were closer to legal sports betting than to investing, and most states outlawed them by the early 1900s. Their rise and fall is a useful reminder that the appearance of broad access is not the same as genuine access: ordinary Americans could put money on stocks long before they could meaningfully own them.

Three structural changes, spread across the second half of the twentieth century, reshaped stock ownership in the United States. The first was **May Day 1975**, the date on which the Securities and Exchange Commission ended the NYSE's fixed-commission system. Commissions, which had been effectively set by the Exchange since 1792, were now negotiable. Within a few years, discount brokerages such as Charles Schwab were offering trades at a fraction of the traditional price, and the economics of retail investing shifted decisively toward lower-cost access. [VERIFY: specific commission decline percentages in the years following May 1, 1975, from SEC rulemaking records.]

The second change was the creation of tax-advantaged retirement accounts. The **Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA)** authorized Individual Retirement Accounts, and Section 401(k), added to the Internal Revenue Code by the Revenue Act of 1978, permitted employer-sponsored defined-contribution plans. By the 1980s, tens of millions of American workers held equities inside retirement accounts for the first time, often without thinking of themselves as "stock investors." [LEGAL CHECK: confirm ERISA (Pub. L. 93-406) and Revenue Act of 1978 §401(k) citations before publication.]

The third change was the invention of the **index fund** (a pooled fund designed to replicate, rather than beat, a market index). John C. Bogle launched the Vanguard 500 Index Trust on August 31, 1976, an offering widely described at the time as unsellable. The first exchange-traded fund, State Street's SPDR S&P 500 Trust, began trading in January 1993. Together, index mutual funds and exchange-traded funds converted stock ownership from a bespoke, research-heavy activity into a near-automatic one: an investor could own a slice of 500 large U.S. companies for a fee of a few basis points per year.

The most recent chapter in this story is the elimination of commissions entirely. In October 2019, Charles Schwab, TD Ameritrade, E*TRADE, and Fidelity each eliminated commissions on U.S. stock and ETF trades, following pressure from newer, app-based entrants such as Robinhood, which had launched commission-free trading in 2013. By the early 2020s, fractional shares allowed a new investor to buy one dollar of almost any public company — a barrier to entry that, measured in real terms, has arguably never been lower in the history of American equity markets.

The arc of stock access, then, is one of steady democratization: from private cartel to retail brokerage to retirement-account default to index fund to zero-commission app. None of that erases the risks of the asset class, which later chapters will examine in detail. But it does mean that the "stocks" being compared in this book are no longer the exclusive playground of a professional class. They are available to virtually any U.S. adult with a bank account and a phone.

## The Real Estate Perspective: The Evolution of Real Estate as an Investment

Real estate has been part of American household wealth for much longer than stocks, but its identity as an investment — as opposed to a place to live or a productive plot of land — is largely a twentieth-century development. Four legislative and structural moments carry most of the weight of that transformation.

The starting point is the **Homestead Act** of May 20, 1862, which Abraham Lincoln signed during the Civil War. The Act offered 160 acres of federal land to any adult head of household who would live on it and improve it for five years. By the time the program wound down in the mid-twentieth century, it had transferred roughly 10% of all U.S. land into private ownership. [VERIFY: the 10% figure is widely cited; confirm against the Bureau of Land Management's official tally of patents issued under the Homestead Act.] The Homestead Act established, at a civilizational scale, the American idea that land ownership was a birthright available to ordinary people, not a privilege reserved for the wealthy. That cultural assumption still shapes how Americans think about real estate today.

The second inflection point was the housing finance infrastructure built during and after the Great Depression. The **National Housing Act of 1934** created the Federal Housing Administration, which in turn popularized the long-amortizing, fixed-rate mortgage that is now taken for granted. Before the FHA, most residential mortgages were short-term, interest-only balloons that required refinancing every few years — a structure that collapsed in the early 1930s and left millions of owners insolvent. The **Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944**, known as the G.I. Bill, added Veterans Administration loan guarantees. Together with the creation of Fannie Mae in 1938, these programs gave the United States the most developed long-term mortgage market in the world and turned homeownership into a mass-market proposition. [LEGAL CHECK: confirm 1934 and 1944 statutory citations before publication.]

The third inflection point converted real estate from a direct-ownership asset into a securitized one. The **Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT)** structure was enacted by Congress in 1960 (signed into law by President Eisenhower on September 14, 1960, as Title II of Public Law 86-779). A REIT is a company that owns or finances income-producing real estate and, by statute, distributes at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders; in exchange, it pays no corporate income tax at the entity level [LEGAL CHECK: IRC §856–860 distribution and qualification rules]. REITs allowed ordinary investors to hold real estate through a brokerage account for the first time — a structural precursor to the index fund revolution that would reshape stocks a decade later. The 1970 creation of Ginnie Mae mortgage-backed pass-through securities and the 1970 chartering of Freddie Mac further pulled housing finance into the capital markets.

The fourth inflection point was the rise and fall of deregulated mortgage securitization. Through the 1980s and 1990s, private-label mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs) grew rapidly outside the government-sponsored enterprises. The Savings and Loan crisis of the late 1980s, which prompted Congress to pass the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (FIRREA) in 1989 and create the Resolution Trust Corporation, was an early warning that real estate and credit risk could feed on each other destructively. That warning was not heeded in the run-up to 2008, as the Case Study later in this chapter explores in detail.

The cumulative effect of these changes was to make real estate simultaneously more accessible and more complicated. By the 2020s, an investor could buy direct real estate with a mortgage, buy shares in a publicly traded REIT, invest through a real estate crowdfunding platform, or allocate to a private syndication under SEC Regulation D [LEGAL CHECK: Rule 501 accredited investor thresholds current as of the applicable publication year]. The cultural shorthand of "buying a house" still dominates how Americans talk about real estate, but the actual investment landscape is now considerably broader than that phrase suggests.

According to the Federal Reserve's **Survey of Consumer Finances** (the Federal Reserve's triennial national survey of household balance sheets), the primary residence remained the single largest asset on most middle-income household balance sheets in the most recently published data [VERIFY: confirm exact figures from the most recent SCF; the 2022 wave was the most recent as of publication planning]. That fact is a useful anchor for the rest of this book: for most Americans, the "stocks vs. real estate" question is not theoretical. It is a question about where the next marginal dollar of savings should go, given that they almost certainly already have exposure to one side of the comparison through a home they live in.

## A Century of Returns: Stocks and Real Estate Side by Side

The single most useful long-horizon dataset on the two asset classes is the work of economists Òscar Jordà, Katharina Knoll, Dmitry Kuvshinov, Moritz Schularick, and Alan M. Taylor, published in 2019 in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* under the title "The Rate of Return on Everything, 1870–2015." Using newly assembled historical data from 16 advanced economies over 145 years, the authors compared the real (inflation-adjusted) total returns of equities, housing, short-term bonds, and long-term bonds. Their headline finding surprised many readers: **over the full sample, residential real estate and equities produced very similar average real returns of roughly 7% per year, but housing achieved that return with substantially lower volatility than equities.** [VERIFY: confirm the unweighted real-return averages and volatility figures from Table II of Jordà et al. (2019) before publication.]

For the United States specifically, the historical record is a little different from the global average. The S&P 500 has produced an annualized nominal total return of roughly 10% per year since 1926, or approximately 6.5% to 7% per year after inflation, according to data compiled by NYU Stern's Aswath Damodaran and by Jeremy Siegel in *Stocks for the Long Run* (1994; revised 2022). U.S. residential real estate, measured by the S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index, has produced long-term real appreciation closer to 1% per year — but that figure excludes the rental income (or imputed rent) that a property generates and excludes the return amplification effect of mortgage leverage. On a total-return, leveraged basis, U.S. housing's historical returns are much closer to U.S. equities than the raw price index suggests. [VERIFY: pull specific long-horizon S&P 500 nominal and real annualized returns from Damodaran's updated historical returns spreadsheet at pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar; pull long-term real Case-Shiller appreciation from the S&P Dow Jones Indices methodology document.]

The table below summarizes what the long-horizon data suggests. Readers should note the caveats in the prose below before drawing conclusions from the numbers themselves.

| Factor | Stocks | Real Estate |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Long-term U.S. nominal return (historical average) | ~10% per year (S&P 500, total return, 1926–present) | ~4–5% price appreciation + rental yield [VERIFY specific figures] |
| Long-term global real return (Jordà et al., 1870–2015) | ~7% per year | ~7% per year |
| Relative volatility | Higher | Lower (for direct housing; REITs behave more like equities) |
| Primary return driver | Earnings growth and dividends | Rental income and appreciation; leverage amplification |
| Ease of national data | Very high (daily index pricing) | Moderate (monthly, regional, lagged) |
| Era of mass participation | Post-1975 (May Day, IRA, 401(k), index funds) | Post-1934 (FHA, long-amortizing mortgage, G.I. Bill) |

Three caveats matter before treating this table as a verdict. First, the returns shown are averages over long horizons and mask enormous variation across shorter windows — a point the next section explores. Second, real estate returns are notoriously difficult to measure cleanly because housing is heterogeneous, illiquid, and lightly reported, while stock index data is daily and centralized. Third, long-horizon return data suffers from **survivorship bias** (the tendency of historical datasets to reflect only the markets, companies, and properties that continued to exist, while excluding those that failed) and from changing market structures that make a 2026 investor's experience fundamentally different from a 1926 investor's. Chapter 5 returns to the question of long-horizon returns in far greater depth.

## Historical Inflection Points

Averages hide crises. A chapter on history would be misleading without naming the specific episodes that shaped how stocks and real estate behaved in the United States during the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

**The Great Depression (1929–1932).** The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked at 381.17 on September 3, 1929, and fell to 41.22 on July 8, 1932 — a peak-to-trough decline of approximately 89%. It did not recover its 1929 nominal high until November 1954, roughly a quarter century later. [VERIFY: recovery date of Dow to 1929 nominal peak; widely cited as November 23, 1954.] Residential real estate also declined during the Depression, though regional data is less complete. Foreclosures were mass events and drove the creation of the FHA and the modern mortgage market described earlier.

**The post–World War II housing boom (roughly 1945–1970).** Wartime household savings, the G.I. Bill, FHA insurance, interstate highway construction, and the baby boom combined to produce one of the longest stretches of real, sustained home price appreciation in U.S. history. Suburbanization was, in effect, a national real-estate long position funded partly by federal credit policy.

**The stagflation decade (1973–1982).** The S&P 500 fell by roughly 48% from early 1973 to late 1974 in the worst bear market since the Depression, and inflation reached double digits in the late 1970s. Nominal home prices continued rising through most of this period, and real estate acquired much of its reputation as an "inflation hedge" from this era. Whether that reputation holds under different inflationary regimes is a separate question, which Chapter 5 revisits with fresh data.

**The dot-com bubble (1995–2002).** The NASDAQ Composite peaked at 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, and bottomed at 1,114.11 on October 9, 2002 — a decline of approximately 78%. Residential real estate, by contrast, continued appreciating through the early 2000s, which briefly created the impression that housing was structurally safer than stocks. That impression did not survive the end of the decade.

**The 2008 financial crisis (2007–2009).** Covered in detail in the Case Study below.

**The COVID-19 shock and recovery (2020–2022).** The S&P 500 fell approximately 34% from its February 19, 2020 peak to its March 23, 2020 trough, the fastest bear market on record, and then recovered and surpassed its previous high within six months. U.S. home prices, measured by the Case-Shiller National Index, rose by approximately 40% in the roughly two years between mid-2020 and mid-2022 — one of the most rapid nominal housing appreciation episodes in U.S. history. [VERIFY: exact Case-Shiller National index percentage change, 2020–2022, from S&P Dow Jones Indices.]

These episodes do not average out neatly. They are the texture of the long-run return data. An investor who entered stocks at the 1929 or 1973 or 2000 or 2007 peak experienced a fundamentally different "market" than one who entered five years later. The same is true of real estate. History is not a smooth compounding curve — it is a series of compressed episodes interrupted by long quiet stretches.

## Case Study: The 2008 Financial Crisis

No single event in recent memory is more useful for comparing the two asset classes than the financial crisis of 2007–2009. Stocks and residential real estate were damaged by the same underlying shock — a collapse in U.S. housing credit — and yet the shape, depth, and duration of the damage differed dramatically between them.

**The stock market path.** The S&P 500 reached its pre-crisis closing peak of 1,565.15 on October 9, 2007. Over the next 17 months, it fell to an intraday low and eventual closing trough of 676.53 on March 9, 2009 — a peak-to-trough closing decline of approximately 56.8%. From that trough, the index rose sharply: it regained its October 2007 nominal closing high on March 28, 2013, roughly four years after the bottom and about five and a half years after the original peak. On a total-return basis, including reinvested dividends, the recovery date came somewhat earlier.

**The real estate path.** The S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index had already peaked earlier than stocks, around April 2006, and did not reach its trough until approximately February 2012 — a peak-to-trough decline of roughly 27% in nominal terms [VERIFY: confirm exact peak and trough dates and decline percentage from the S&P Dow Jones Indices Case-Shiller methodology document; some sources cite the trough in March 2012 and declines in the 27% to 33% range depending on the series and whether inflation-adjusted]. The nominal recovery to the 2006 peak did not arrive until late 2016, roughly a decade after the original peak and nearly five years after the trough. In inflation-adjusted terms, the recovery took even longer.

**The comparison.** On the surface, the raw peak-to-trough numbers favor real estate: a 27% decline is less frightening than a 57% decline. But that comparison is misleading for three reasons, each of which foreshadows later chapters. First, real estate in 2008 was held almost entirely with **leverage** (borrowed money, typically a mortgage; see Chapter 11), and a 27% decline in home value can easily translate into a 100% decline in equity for a leveraged owner — and foreclosure for an owner who cannot make payments. Stock investors, by contrast, typically held equities without margin loans, so a 57% market decline translated into a 57% portfolio decline rather than a wipeout. Second, real estate losses were geographically concentrated: homeowners in Phoenix, Las Vegas, Miami, and parts of California experienced declines far larger than 27%, while homeowners in some other metropolitan areas experienced far smaller ones. The national average is genuinely national and obscures this local variation. Third, the recovery paths had very different shapes: stocks fell faster, fell further, and recovered faster; real estate fell more slowly, fell less, and recovered much more slowly. For an investor whose time horizon ended during the downturn — someone who had to sell to fund a retirement, a tuition bill, or a medical expense — the stock-market path was more frightening but the real-estate path was more permanent.

The 2008 episode also illustrates the importance of **correlation** (the degree to which two assets move together). In ordinary conditions, stocks and residential real estate are only loosely correlated. In a severe credit crisis, they can move sharply in the same direction at the same time, which is the single most uncomfortable moment for a diversified investor. Chapter 15's discussion of combined portfolios returns to this point.

The durable lesson of 2008 is not that one asset class was "safer" than the other. Both were damaged, badly, by the same underlying shock. The lesson is that the *manner* of damage differed — speed, depth, leverage exposure, recovery timeline — and those differences mattered more to real investors than any single headline percentage. Every dimension compared in Part II of this book was affected by 2008 in some way, and every dimension recovered on its own schedule.

## What History Teaches — And What It Does Not

History teaches that both asset classes, over long enough horizons, have rewarded patient capital. It teaches that access to both has expanded dramatically, that institutional structures matter, and that the worst episodes are rarely predicted by the averages. It also teaches that the two asset classes, while often described as alternatives, have sometimes moved together and sometimes apart — a fact that later chapters on risk and portfolio construction will examine closely.

History does *not* teach that past returns will repeat. Market structures change. Interest-rate regimes change. Tax rules change. Demographics change. Technology changes what an "investment" even means. The Homestead Act and the Buttonwood Agreement both still shape American finance, but neither is a model for how to invest in 2026. Every chapter that follows treats historical data as evidence to be weighed, not prophecy to be followed.

## Key Takeaways

- Stocks and residential real estate have produced broadly similar long-run real returns across advanced economies, but they have done so through very different paths and with very different volatility.
- Mass participation in the stock market is largely a post-1975 phenomenon built on deregulated commissions, tax-advantaged retirement accounts, index funds, and, most recently, commission-free fractional trading.
- Mass participation in real estate as an investment, rather than simply as shelter, was built on a twentieth-century scaffolding of federal mortgage insurance, long-amortizing loans, and the REIT structure created in 1960.
- The 2008 financial crisis damaged both asset classes through the same underlying shock but produced different shapes of decline and recovery; leverage and geography mattered as much as the headline percentages.
- Long-horizon return averages hide decade-long episodes of crisis and boom, and any historical comparison of stocks and real estate must account for survivorship bias, changing market structures, and the limits of what past data can predict.
