## Introduction

Chapter 1 traced the parallel histories of stocks and real estate from 1792 to the present. It established that both asset classes have, over very long horizons, produced broadly similar real total returns, and that each has reshaped itself through waves of deregulation, technology, and financial innovation. Before the two can be compared on any honest footing, however, each needs to be understood on its own terms. Part I of this book is devoted to that task. Chapter 2 takes the first asset class, publicly traded equities, and explains what a stock actually is, how the markets that price and trade stocks function, and what a modern investor is really buying when the words "I own shares" come out of someone's mouth.

The framing matters because stocks are unusually abstract. A share certificate, even in the rare case that a physical one still exists, is not the business. It is a legal entitlement: a claim on a slice of whatever the business owns after its debts are paid, a claim on whatever income the business decides to distribute, and a vote on who runs it. That abstraction is both the asset's greatest strength and its greatest source of confusion. Real estate, by contrast, is concrete: a deed points to a parcel, a parcel holds a structure, a structure houses a tenant, a tenant pays rent. Chapter 3 will examine that side of the ledger in equal depth. This chapter focuses only on stocks, and it begins with the most basic and most frequently glossed-over question of all: what is a shareholder actually entitled to?

## What Buying a Stock Actually Means

A **common stock** (a unit of residual ownership in a corporation) represents a fractional claim on a business. When an investor purchases one share of Apple, Microsoft, or a regional community bank, that investor becomes a partial owner of the entire enterprise, entitled, along with every other shareholder, to a proportional share of whatever the company produces. The word "residual" is central to the definition, because it captures the legal order of priority that governs every public company: employees are paid, suppliers are paid, lenders are paid, bondholders are paid, and preferred shareholders are paid, and only then do common shareholders receive whatever remains. In a thriving company, what remains can be extraordinary. In a failing one, it can be nothing at all.

This residual claim takes three practical forms. The first is a claim on current earnings. When a company generates net income, the board of directors may choose to distribute a portion of that income to shareholders in the form of a **dividend** (a cash or share payment issued to shareholders out of corporate earnings) or to retain the earnings and reinvest them in the business. Either choice, in principle, accrues to the shareholder: a distributed dollar arrives in a brokerage account, and a retained dollar, if invested at a competitive rate of return, raises the long-run value of the share itself. The second form is a claim on the assets of the company in the event of liquidation. If a corporation is wound up, whether voluntarily or through bankruptcy, its assets are sold, its creditors are paid in statutory order, and only the residual value, the amount left after every senior claimant is satisfied, flows to common shareholders. The third form is a claim on control, exercised through the shareholder vote.

Voting rights are the governance side of common stock. Each common share typically carries one vote on matters put to the shareholders, including the election of directors, the approval of auditors, the ratification of executive compensation plans, and, in the case of major transactions, the approval of mergers or sales of the company. In practice, the influence of any single retail investor's vote is negligible, but the aggregate of shareholder votes is what disciplines boards and, through them, management. A shareholder who holds one share and a shareholder who holds one million shares have proportionally identical economic and voting rights per share; what differs is the absolute number of votes each can cast. Dual-class share structures, increasingly common among newly listed technology companies, complicate this picture by issuing high-vote shares to founders and low-vote or non-voting shares to the public, but the underlying framework of residual ownership remains intact.

**Preferred stock** (a class of equity that sits between bonds and common stock in the capital structure) differs from common stock in two important ways. First, it usually pays a fixed dividend, set when the shares are issued, and that dividend must be paid in full before any dividend can be distributed to common shareholders. Second, in the event of liquidation, preferred shareholders stand ahead of common shareholders in line, though still behind bondholders and other creditors. In exchange for that seniority, preferred shareholders generally give up voting rights and, more importantly, give up most of the upside: a preferred share's price tends to behave more like a bond than like a growth asset, rising and falling with interest rates rather than with the fortunes of the underlying business. For the purposes of this book, the word "stock," when unqualified, refers to common stock, because common stock is the vehicle through which the overwhelming majority of retail investors participate in the equity markets.

The implication of all of this is that buying a stock is not buying a ticker symbol or a price chart. It is buying a fractional share of a legal entity whose earnings, assets, and governance all belong, in part, to the buyer. Everything that follows in this chapter, and everything that Part II of this book compares against real estate, rests on that foundation.

## Types of Stocks

Not all common stocks behave the same way, and professional investors have developed a handful of overlapping classification schemes to describe the differences. None of these categories is an official legal designation; they are analytical shortcuts that help investors organize the universe of listed equities into groups with broadly similar characteristics.

The first and most basic split is by market capitalization. **Market capitalization** (the total dollar value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated by multiplying the current share price by the number of shares outstanding) is the standard measure of a company's size in the public markets. By convention, companies with market capitalizations above roughly $10 billion are described as large-cap, those between roughly $2 billion and $10 billion as mid-cap, and those between roughly $250 million and $2 billion as small-cap. Micro-cap and nano-cap tiers sit below that threshold. The thresholds are informal and differ slightly between index providers, but the general ordering is consistent across the industry. Size matters because it correlates, however loosely, with the maturity of a business, the depth of trading in its shares, the scrutiny it receives from analysts, and the volatility of its price.

A second split is by style: growth versus value. A **growth stock** is a share in a company whose revenues, earnings, or both are expected to expand materially faster than the broader market average, often at the cost of current profitability. A **value stock** is a share in a company whose market price appears low relative to a fundamental measure such as book value, earnings, or cash flow, typically because the business is mature, cyclical, or out of favor. Both styles have produced long periods of outperformance and long periods of underperformance, and the academic literature has spent decades debating whether the value premium documented by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French in the early 1990s reflects a durable reward for risk or simply a historical artifact. For the introductory purposes of this chapter, the takeaway is narrower: growth and value describe two distinct, widely tracked slices of the equity market, and most index providers publish separate growth and value indexes for each capitalization tier.

A third split is by geography. Domestic stocks, for a United States investor, are shares listed on U.S. exchanges and denominated in U.S. dollars. International stocks, sometimes labeled developed-market or emerging-market depending on the classification of the country, are shares in companies headquartered and primarily traded abroad. International exposure has historically been justified on diversification grounds, because equity markets in different countries do not move in perfect lockstep, but the diversification benefit has narrowed since the 1990s as global capital markets have become more tightly integrated.

A fourth split is by sector. Index providers, most prominently S&P Dow Jones Indices and MSCI through the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), divide the equity market into eleven broad sectors: information technology, health care, financials, consumer discretionary, communication services, industrials, consumer staples, energy, utilities, real estate, and materials. Sector classification allows investors to see how exposure is distributed across different parts of the economy, and it is the basis for the many sector-specific exchange-traded funds discussed later in this chapter. Notably, real estate was carved out of the financials sector as its own GICS headline sector in 2016, a recognition by the index providers that publicly traded real estate investment trusts had grown large enough and distinct enough to deserve standalone classification. That reclassification is a small but telling piece of evidence for the central premise of this book: the two asset classes at the heart of this comparison are increasingly treated as peers, even by the institutions that build the benchmarks.

Investors should also note that these classifications are overlapping rather than mutually exclusive. A single share can simultaneously be a large-cap, value, domestic, financial-sector stock. The categories are lenses, not boxes, and the purpose of naming them here is to introduce vocabulary that will recur throughout the book.

## Dividend Stocks and Income Investing

One of the most durable reasons to own stocks is income. Dividends, the cash payments that corporations distribute to shareholders out of earnings, have historically accounted for a meaningful share of total equity returns. According to the NYU Stern historical returns dataset compiled by Aswath Damodaran, roughly one-third of the long-run total return on the S&P 500 since 1928 has come from dividends and their reinvestment, with the remainder from price appreciation.[^1] That proportion has varied sharply across decades, with dividends accounting for the majority of total return in the 1940s and 1970s and for a much smaller share during the 1990s and 2010s, but the long-run contribution is large enough that any discussion of stock returns that ignores dividends is incomplete.

A dividend is not a legal obligation. Unlike a bond coupon, which a company must pay on schedule or default, a dividend is declared at the discretion of the board of directors and can be raised, lowered, suspended, or eliminated at any time. That discretion is both a feature and a risk. It is a feature because it gives management flexibility to preserve cash during downturns rather than going into debt to maintain a payout. It is a risk because a dividend that looks generous on paper can disappear without warning, as happened across large swathes of the S&P 500 during the 2008 financial crisis and again, on a smaller scale, during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

Two metrics dominate the analysis of dividend stocks. The first is the **dividend yield** (the annual dividend per share divided by the current share price, expressed as a percentage), which tells an investor how much income the stock generates relative to its market value. A share trading at $100 that pays $3 per year in dividends offers a 3% dividend yield. The yield moves inversely with price: if the share falls to $75 without a change in the dividend, the yield rises to 4%, and if the share rises to $150, the yield falls to 2%. The second is the **dividend payout ratio** (the share of a company's net income paid out as dividends), which measures how sustainable the dividend is. A payout ratio of 30% means the company is distributing thirty cents of every dollar of earnings and retaining the rest; a payout ratio above 100% means the company is paying out more than it earns and is likely funding the dividend from cash reserves or borrowings. Neither metric is useful on its own. A high yield paired with a payout ratio above 100% often signals a dividend cut is imminent; a low yield paired with a low payout ratio may signal room to grow the distribution.

Investors who prioritize dividend income often look to a small subset of the market informally known as the **Dividend Aristocrats** (S&P 500 constituents that have raised their dividend every year for at least 25 consecutive years). The index, maintained by S&P Dow Jones Indices, has historically been populated by large, mature consumer staples, industrials, and health care companies. Membership is rare and is lost immediately if a company cuts or freezes its dividend. A closely related list, the Dividend Kings, requires 50 consecutive years of increases. Neither list is a guarantee of future performance, and the composition of both has shifted meaningfully over the past two decades as some long-standing members have been acquired, restructured, or forced to cut during recessions. But together they illustrate a useful distinction: between a dividend that exists and a dividend that has been raised, through multiple economic cycles, by a management team committed to returning capital to shareholders.

A final mechanic worth introducing here is the **dividend reinvestment plan** (commonly abbreviated DRIP), a feature offered by most major brokerages and many company-sponsored share plans that automatically uses each dividend payment to purchase additional shares of the issuing company, often fractional shares, without commission. DRIPs are the practical expression of the reinvestment assumption that underlies every total-return calculation. Over multi-decade horizons, the compounding effect of reinvested dividends is substantial. The same $10,000 invested in the S&P 500 at the start of 1990 and held through 2024 would, according to standard total-return index data, have produced roughly three to four times the terminal wealth in the reinvestment case compared with the price-only case. That gap is not a rounding error; it is the core of the long-run equity compounding story.

## How Stock Exchanges Work

A stock exchange is, at its most basic, a regulated venue where buyers and sellers of listed securities meet. In the United States, two exchanges dominate the listing of corporate equities. The **New York Stock Exchange** (NYSE), founded in the years following the 1792 Buttonwood Agreement discussed in Chapter 1, is the oldest U.S. exchange and, by the market capitalization of its listed companies, the largest in the world. The **NASDAQ Stock Market**, founded on February 8, 1971 by the National Association of Securities Dealers, was the world's first fully electronic stock market; it began life as an automated quotation system, with trade execution still conducted over the phone, and did not become a fully automated trading venue until 1984.[^2] Intel, which listed on NASDAQ in October 1971, was the first high-profile technology issuer and set a template that Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Google, and Meta would later follow.[^3] Between them, the NYSE and NASDAQ list the overwhelming majority of publicly traded U.S. companies. According to World Bank and industry data compiled through 2024, there were roughly 4,000 listed domestic companies on U.S. exchanges, a figure that has declined from a peak of more than 7,000 in the late 1990s as mergers, going-private transactions, and a slowdown in initial public offerings have contracted the public universe.[^4]

The mechanics of how trades are matched have changed dramatically since the early days of both exchanges. On the historical NYSE, orders flowed to a single human **specialist** (the designated market maker responsible for maintaining a fair and orderly market in a particular listed stock) who matched buy and sell orders on the physical trading floor. On NASDAQ, multiple **market makers** (dealer firms that stand ready to buy or sell a given security at publicly quoted bid and ask prices) competed to provide liquidity in each listed name. Both models still technically exist in vestigial form, but the dominant execution venues today are electronic order books, often called matching engines, that process orders in microseconds. When a retail investor places a trade through a discount broker, the order is typically routed not directly to the listing exchange but to a wholesale market maker that fills the order from its own inventory, a practice known as payment for order flow. The details of that routing are disclosed in quarterly reports that brokers are required to file under SEC Rule 606.

Investors communicate their intentions through a small vocabulary of standardized order types. A **market order** instructs the broker to buy or sell at the best currently available price, without regard to what that price turns out to be. A **limit order** instructs the broker to buy only at or below a specified maximum price, or to sell only at or above a specified minimum price, and otherwise to do nothing. A **stop-loss order** instructs the broker to convert a position into a market order once the share price crosses a specified trigger level, a tool commonly used to cap downside losses on an existing holding. Variants of each, including stop-limit orders, trailing stops, and fill-or-kill orders, extend the basic set but do not alter the underlying logic. The choice of order type is not trivial: in a fast-moving market, a market order offers speed at the risk of an unfavorable fill, while a limit order offers price control at the risk of no fill at all.

Trading hours for U.S. listed stocks are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time on business days, with pre-market and after-hours electronic sessions extending the window at both ends. The official closing auction, conducted in the final seconds of the regular session, determines the day's closing price and is one of the largest concentrated liquidity events in global finance. Once a trade is executed, the transaction enters a settlement cycle during which cash and shares are formally exchanged. For decades, the U.S. market operated on a T+2 cycle, meaning that settlement occurred two business days after the trade. On May 28, 2024, the Securities and Exchange Commission's shortened settlement rule took effect, moving U.S. equity trades to a **T+1 cycle** (settlement one business day after the trade date).[^5] The shortened cycle reduces counterparty risk and aligns the United States with several other major markets. For a retail investor, T+1 means that cash from a sale becomes available for withdrawal one day sooner than under the previous regime, a modest but meaningful improvement in capital efficiency.

## Index Funds, ETFs, and Mutual Funds

If the invention of the common stock gave ordinary investors a claim on a business, the invention of the pooled investment fund gave them a claim on the entire market. A **mutual fund** (an open-end investment company that pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of securities) has existed in the United States since the 1920s, but its dominance over retail investing dates from the 1970s and 1980s, when rising personal incomes, the launch of the 401(k), and the growth of IRAs channeled trillions of dollars into pooled vehicles. A mutual fund issues shares directly to investors at the fund's **net asset value** (NAV), calculated once per day after the close of trading, and redeems shares at the NAV computed on the day the redemption request is received. That once-a-day pricing model is administratively simple but operationally rigid, and it was this rigidity that set the stage for the exchange-traded fund.

An **exchange-traded fund** (ETF) is a pooled investment vehicle whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the trading day at prices determined by supply and demand rather than by end-of-day NAV. The mechanism that keeps the ETF's market price in line with the value of its underlying holdings is a creation-and-redemption process involving large institutional participants known as authorized participants, who can exchange baskets of the underlying securities for new ETF shares, or ETF shares for the underlying securities, in very large blocks. The result is a vehicle that combines the diversification of a mutual fund with the intraday liquidity of a single stock. As noted in Chapter 1, the first U.S. ETF, the SPDR S&P 500 Trust, began trading on the American Stock Exchange on January 22, 1993. In the three decades that followed, the ETF structure has expanded from a single product tracking the S&P 500 into a universe of several thousand funds spanning virtually every public asset class.

The single most important variant of both mutual funds and ETFs, for long-run retail investors, is the **index fund** (a pooled vehicle that seeks to replicate, rather than beat, the performance of a specified market index). The intellectual case for index investing was made by academics in the 1960s and 1970s, and the commercial case was proven, as noted in Chapter 1, by John Bogle's launch of the First Index Investment Trust in August 1976. For the first two decades of its existence, index investing was a fringe strategy. By the early 2000s it had become a credible alternative to active management. By the early 2020s it had become the dominant form of equity investing in the United States. According to the Investment Company Institute's 2024 Fact Book, index mutual funds and index ETFs together held roughly 48 percent of long-term fund assets at the end of 2023, and index equity funds accounted for 57 percent of all equity fund assets by October 2024, up from 20 percent in 2012.[^6]

The reason for that extraordinary shift is simple and, at this point, uncontroversial: cost. Every dollar paid in fund fees is a dollar deducted from the investor's return, every year, for as long as the position is held. The ICI reports that the asset-weighted average expense ratio on actively managed equity mutual funds in 2023 was 0.64 percent, while the asset-weighted average on index equity mutual funds was 0.05 percent.[^7] Expressed as basis points, the gap is roughly sixty. Compounded over a thirty-year holding period, a sixty-basis-point annual fee drag reduces terminal wealth by roughly 16 to 18 percent relative to a zero-fee benchmark, a finding that is straightforward to verify with any compound-interest calculator. That arithmetic, more than any philosophical argument, is what has driven the migration of retail capital out of high-fee active products and into low-fee index products over the past two decades.

The practical universe of index funds available to a modern retail investor includes **total market funds** that attempt to capture every listed stock in a given country, **broad index funds** that track well-known benchmarks such as the S&P 500 or the Russell 2000, **international funds** that cover developed or emerging foreign markets, and **sector funds** that concentrate on a single GICS sector. The proliferation of these products means that a retail investor can, for a total expense ratio of well under 10 basis points, assemble a globally diversified equity portfolio that would have been essentially unavailable at any price to an individual investor a generation ago. That democratization of access is, in many ways, the single most important development in equity investing since the end of fixed commissions.

## Modern Access Points

The path by which an individual actually owns a share of stock in 2026 bears little resemblance to the paper-certificate era that persisted well into the second half of the twentieth century. Three changes stand out. The first is **zero commissions**, which, as described in Chapter 1, became the industry standard for U.S. retail brokers in October 2019. The second is **fractional shares**, a feature offered by all of the major discount brokers, which allows an investor to buy a dollar amount of a stock rather than a whole number of shares. Fractional share availability means that a $100 investment can purchase a proportional slice of a company whose share price is $500 or $5,000 without any meaningful loss of diversification. The third is **mobile-first trading**, delivered through smartphone applications that have pushed account opening, funding, trading, and research into a single device. In 2024, Gallup reported that 62 percent of Americans owned stock in some form, a figure that rises to 87 percent for households earning $100,000 or more per year and falls to 28 percent for households earning less than $50,000.[^8] Participation is still unevenly distributed across the income spectrum, but the raw friction to getting started, once counted in weeks and hundreds of dollars, has collapsed to minutes and zero.

Robo-advisors, automated portfolio construction services that build and maintain low-cost, diversified portfolios based on a short investor questionnaire, are a fourth access point worth mentioning here only briefly. Chapter 15, "Tools, Technology, and Platforms," treats them in depth. For the purposes of this foundational chapter, the relevant point is that the infrastructure of equity investing has been flattened to the point where an investor of any experience level can, in principle, establish a diversified position in the U.S. or global stock market for a few dollars and a few minutes of setup. Whether that ease is a good thing, and what risks it introduces, is a subject for Part III of this book.

## Key Stock Metrics Introduced

Every asset class has a short list of quantitative metrics that recur in almost every analysis. For publicly traded equities, five are essential, and each is defined briefly here so that later chapters can use the terminology without interruption.

The **price-to-earnings ratio** (P/E ratio) is the current share price divided by the company's earnings per share over a trailing or forward twelve-month period. It is the most widely cited valuation measure in the equity markets. A P/E of 20 means an investor is paying twenty dollars for every dollar of annual earnings the company is generating; all else equal, lower ratios indicate cheaper valuations and higher ratios indicate more expensive ones. The ratio is not comparable across sectors without adjustment, and it breaks down entirely for companies with negative earnings, but it remains the default first glance at whether a stock is priced richly or modestly relative to its own history and its peers.

**Earnings per share** (EPS) is the company's net income divided by its average number of diluted shares outstanding. EPS is the numerator-side input to the P/E ratio and the measure against which Wall Street analyst estimates are most often expressed. Companies typically report both a basic and a diluted EPS figure, with diluted EPS accounting for the dilutive effect of stock options, restricted stock units, and convertible securities that would, if exercised or converted, increase the share count.

**Market capitalization**, defined earlier in this chapter, is the simplest measure of company size and the basis for the capitalization weighting used by the S&P 500, the Russell 3000, the MSCI ACWI, and most other widely followed equity benchmarks. In a cap-weighted index, each constituent's contribution to the index return is proportional to its market capitalization, which is why the performance of the largest handful of U.S. technology companies has exerted an outsized influence on S&P 500 returns in recent years.

**Dividend yield**, also defined earlier in this chapter, measures current income relative to price. Its complement, the **total yield**, adds to the dividend yield the percentage of shares the company repurchases in a given year through buybacks, on the theory that a dollar returned through a repurchase has the same economic effect as a dollar returned through a dividend. Total yield has become a more common metric since the 2010s as share repurchases have grown relative to dividends in aggregate.

**Beta**, finally, is a measure of a stock's price volatility relative to the broader market, typically the S&P 500 by convention. A beta of 1.0 implies the stock tends to move in line with the market, a beta above 1.0 implies greater volatility, and a beta below 1.0 implies lesser volatility. Beta is an input to the Capital Asset Pricing Model, which is examined in more depth in Chapter 14, and it is an imperfect but widely used summary of how a single stock's risk compares with the market as a whole.

## Side-by-Side Setup

This chapter has deliberately refrained from comparing stocks with real estate. The purpose of Part I is to build two self-contained portraits, one in this chapter and one in the next, before Part II sets them side by side. ## Key Takeaways

- A common stock is a residual claim on a business, entitling the shareholder to a proportional share of earnings, assets, and governance, only after every senior claimant has been paid.
- Equity markets classify stocks by market capitalization, style (growth or value), geography, and GICS sector; these categories overlap, and a single share can belong to several at once.
- Dividends have historically accounted for a substantial share of long-run equity total returns, but they are declared at the discretion of the board of directors and can be cut or suspended without notice.
- U.S. listed stocks now trade primarily through electronic order books, settle on a T+1 cycle as of May 28, 2024, and can be transacted at zero commission through any major discount broker.
- Index mutual funds and index ETFs accounted for roughly 48 percent of long-term fund assets by year-end 2023 and 57 percent of equity fund assets by October 2024, a shift driven principally by the large and compounding cost advantage of passive over active management.
- The essential quantitative vocabulary of equity analysis, which recurs throughout the rest of the book, includes the P/E ratio, earnings per share, market capitalization, dividend yield, and beta.
