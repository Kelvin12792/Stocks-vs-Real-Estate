## Introduction

Chapter 1 traced the parallel histories of stocks and real estate and established that both asset classes have, over very long horizons, produced broadly similar real total returns. Before the two can be compared on any honest footing, each needs to be understood on its own terms. Chapter 2 takes the first asset class, publicly traded equities, and explains what a stock actually is, how the markets that price and trade stocks function, and what a modern investor is really buying when the words "I own shares" come out of someone's mouth.

The framing matters because stocks are unusually abstract. A share certificate is not the business. It is a legal entitlement: a claim on a slice of whatever the business owns after its debts are paid, a claim on whatever income the business decides to distribute, and a vote on who runs it. Real estate, by contrast, is concrete: a deed points to a parcel, a parcel holds a structure, a structure houses a tenant, a tenant pays rent. Chapter 3 will take that side of the ledger. This chapter focuses on stocks, and it begins with the most basic question of all: what is a shareholder actually entitled to?

## What Buying a Stock Actually Means

A **common stock** (a unit of residual ownership in a corporation) represents a fractional claim on a business. When an investor purchases one share of Apple, Microsoft, or a regional community bank, that investor becomes a partial owner of the entire enterprise, entitled to a proportional share of whatever the company produces. The word "residual" is central, because it captures the legal order of priority: employees, suppliers, lenders, bondholders, and preferred shareholders are paid first, and only then do common shareholders receive whatever remains. In a thriving company, what remains can be extraordinary. In a failing one, it can be nothing at all.

This residual claim takes three practical forms. The first is a claim on current earnings, which the board may distribute as a **dividend** (a cash or share payment issued to shareholders out of corporate earnings) or retain and reinvest in the business. Either choice accrues to the shareholder: a distributed dollar arrives in a brokerage account, and a retained dollar, if invested at a competitive return, raises the long-run value of the share. The second is a claim on the assets of the company in the event of liquidation: if the corporation is wound up, assets are sold, creditors are paid in statutory order, and only the residual flows to common shareholders. The third is a claim on control, exercised through the shareholder vote.

Voting rights are the governance side of common stock. Each common share typically carries one vote on matters put to the shareholders, including the election of directors, the approval of auditors, executive compensation, and major transactions such as mergers. In practice the influence of any single retail investor's vote is negligible, but the aggregate of shareholder votes is what disciplines boards and, through them, management. Dual-class share structures, increasingly common among newly listed technology companies, complicate this picture by issuing high-vote shares to founders and low-vote or non-voting shares to the public, but the underlying framework of residual ownership remains intact.

**Preferred stock** (a class of equity that sits between bonds and common stock in the capital structure) differs in two important ways. First, it usually pays a fixed dividend that must be paid in full before any dividend can be distributed to common shareholders. Second, in liquidation, preferred shareholders stand ahead of common shareholders, though still behind bondholders. In exchange for that seniority, preferred shareholders generally give up voting rights and most of the upside: a preferred share tends to behave more like a bond than like a growth asset. For the purposes of this book, the unqualified word "stock" refers to common stock, the vehicle through which the overwhelming majority of retail investors participate in the equity markets.

The implication is that buying a stock is not buying a ticker symbol or a price chart. It is buying a fractional share of a legal entity whose earnings, assets, and governance all belong, in part, to the buyer.

## Types of Stocks

Not all common stocks behave the same way, and professional investors use a handful of overlapping classification schemes to describe the differences. None of these categories is an official legal designation; they are analytical shortcuts for organizing the universe of listed equities into groups with broadly similar characteristics.

The first split is by size. **Market capitalization** (the total dollar value of a company's outstanding shares, calculated by multiplying the current share price by the number of shares outstanding) is the standard measure. By convention, companies above roughly $10 billion are described as large-cap, those between $2 billion and $10 billion as mid-cap, and those between $250 million and $2 billion as small-cap, with micro-cap and nano-cap tiers below. The thresholds differ slightly between index providers, but the ordering is consistent. Size correlates loosely with the maturity of the business, the depth of trading in its shares, and the volatility of its price.

A second split is by style. A **growth stock** is a share in a company whose revenues or earnings are expected to expand materially faster than the broader market, often at the cost of current profitability. A **value stock** is a share whose market price appears low relative to a fundamental measure such as book value, earnings, or cash flow, typically because the business is mature, cyclical, or out of favor. The academic literature has debated for decades whether the value premium documented by Fama and French in the early 1990s reflects a durable reward for risk, but for the introductory purposes of this chapter the takeaway is narrower: growth and value describe two widely tracked slices of the equity market, and most index providers publish separate growth and value indexes for each capitalization tier.

A third split is by geography. Domestic stocks, for a U.S. investor, are shares listed on U.S. exchanges. International stocks, labeled developed-market or emerging-market depending on the country, are shares in companies headquartered and primarily traded abroad. International exposure has been justified on diversification grounds, though the diversification benefit has narrowed since the 1990s as global capital markets have become more tightly integrated.

A fourth split is by sector. Through the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), S&P Dow Jones Indices and MSCI divide the equity market into eleven broad sectors: information technology, health care, financials, consumer discretionary, communication services, industrials, consumer staples, energy, utilities, real estate, and materials. Real estate was carved out of the financials sector as its own GICS headline sector in 2016, a recognition that publicly traded REITs had grown large enough to deserve standalone classification. That reclassification is a small but telling piece of evidence for the central premise of this book: the two asset classes at the heart of this comparison are increasingly treated as peers, even by the institutions that build the benchmarks.

These classifications are overlapping rather than mutually exclusive. A single share can simultaneously be a large-cap, value, domestic, financial-sector stock. The categories are lenses, not boxes.

## Dividend Stocks and Income Investing

One of the most durable reasons to own stocks is income. Dividends, the cash payments that corporations distribute to shareholders out of earnings, have historically accounted for a meaningful share of total equity returns. According to the NYU Stern historical returns dataset compiled by Aswath Damodaran, roughly one-third of the long-run total return on the S&P 500 since 1928 has come from dividends and their reinvestment, with the remainder from price appreciation.[^1] The long-run contribution is large enough that any discussion of stock returns that ignores dividends is incomplete.

A dividend is not a legal obligation. Unlike a bond coupon, which a company must pay on schedule or default, a dividend is declared at the discretion of the board of directors and can be raised, lowered, suspended, or eliminated at any time. That discretion gives management flexibility to preserve cash during downturns, but it also means a dividend that looks generous on paper can disappear without warning, as happened across large swathes of the S&P 500 during the 2008 financial crisis and again during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.

Two metrics dominate the analysis of dividend stocks. The first is the **dividend yield** (the annual dividend per share divided by the current share price, expressed as a percentage). A share trading at $100 that pays $3 per year in dividends offers a 3% yield, and the yield moves inversely with price. The second is the **dividend payout ratio** (the share of a company's net income paid out as dividends), which measures how sustainable the dividend is. A payout ratio above 100% means the company is paying out more than it earns and is likely funding the dividend from cash reserves or borrowings. Neither metric is useful on its own: a high yield paired with a payout ratio above 100% often signals a dividend cut is imminent; a low yield paired with a low payout ratio may signal room to grow.

Investors who prioritize dividend income often look to a subset of the market known as the **Dividend Aristocrats** (S&P 500 constituents that have raised their dividend every year for at least 25 consecutive years). Membership is rare and is lost immediately if a company cuts or freezes its dividend. A related list, the Dividend Kings, requires 50 consecutive years of increases. Together they illustrate a useful distinction between a dividend that exists and a dividend that has been raised through multiple economic cycles by a management team committed to returning capital to shareholders.

A final mechanic worth introducing is the **dividend reinvestment plan** (DRIP), a feature offered by most major brokerages that automatically uses each dividend payment to purchase additional shares, often fractional, without commission. DRIPs are the practical expression of the reinvestment assumption that underlies every total-return calculation, and over multi-decade horizons the compounding effect is substantial. The gap between price-only and total-return S&P 500 performance is not a rounding error; it is the core of the long-run equity compounding story.

## How Stock Exchanges Work

A stock exchange is a regulated venue where buyers and sellers of listed securities meet. In the United States, two exchanges dominate. The **New York Stock Exchange** (NYSE), founded in the years following the 1792 Buttonwood Agreement, is the oldest U.S. exchange and, by the market capitalization of its listed companies, the largest in the world. The **NASDAQ Stock Market**, founded on February 8, 1971 by the National Association of Securities Dealers, was the world's first fully electronic stock market; it began as an automated quotation system, with trade execution still conducted over the phone, and did not become a fully automated trading venue until 1984.[^2] Intel, which listed on NASDAQ in October 1971, was the first high-profile technology issuer and set a template that Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Google, and Meta would later follow.[^3] According to World Bank and industry data compiled through 2024, there were roughly 4,000 listed domestic companies on U.S. exchanges, down from a peak of more than 7,000 in the late 1990s as mergers, going-private transactions, and a slowdown in IPOs have contracted the public universe.[^4]

The mechanics of how trades are matched have changed dramatically. On the historical NYSE, orders flowed to a single human **specialist** (the designated market maker responsible for maintaining a fair and orderly market in a listed stock) on the physical trading floor. On NASDAQ, multiple **market makers** (dealer firms that stand ready to buy or sell a security at publicly quoted bid and ask prices) competed to provide liquidity. Today the dominant execution venues are electronic order books that process orders in microseconds, and retail orders are typically routed to wholesale market makers that fill from their own inventory under the practice of payment for order flow, disclosed quarterly under SEC Rule 606.

Investors communicate their intentions through a small vocabulary of standardized order types. A **market order** instructs the broker to buy or sell at the best currently available price. A **limit order** instructs the broker to buy only at or below a specified maximum price, or to sell only at or above a specified minimum. A **stop-loss order** converts a position into a market order once the share price crosses a specified trigger, a tool commonly used to cap downside losses. In a fast-moving market, a market order offers speed at the risk of an unfavorable fill, while a limit order offers price control at the risk of no fill at all.

Trading hours for U.S. listed stocks are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time, with pre-market and after-hours electronic sessions extending the window. Once a trade is executed, it enters a settlement cycle. For decades the U.S. market operated on T+2. On May 28, 2024, the Securities and Exchange Commission's shortened settlement rule took effect, moving U.S. equity trades to a **T+1 cycle** (settlement one business day after the trade date).[^5] The shortened cycle reduces counterparty risk and aligns the United States with several other major markets.

## Index Funds, ETFs, and Mutual Funds

If the common stock gave ordinary investors a claim on a business, the pooled investment fund gave them a claim on the entire market. A **mutual fund** (an open-end investment company that pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of securities) has existed in the United States since the 1920s, but its dominance over retail investing dates from the 1970s and 1980s, when the launch of the 401(k) and the growth of IRAs channeled trillions of dollars into pooled vehicles. A mutual fund issues and redeems shares directly at the fund's **net asset value** (NAV), calculated once per day after the close of trading. That once-a-day pricing model is administratively simple but operationally rigid, and it was this rigidity that set the stage for the exchange-traded fund.

An **exchange-traded fund** (ETF) is a pooled investment vehicle whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the trading day at market-determined prices rather than at end-of-day NAV. A creation-and-redemption process involving authorized participants, who can exchange baskets of the underlying securities for new ETF shares or vice versa in very large blocks, keeps the ETF's market price in line with the value of its underlying holdings. The result is a vehicle that combines the diversification of a mutual fund with the intraday liquidity of a single stock. As noted in Chapter 1, the first U.S. ETF, the SPDR S&P 500 Trust, began trading in January 1993. The ETF structure has since expanded into a universe of several thousand funds spanning virtually every public asset class.

The single most important variant, for long-run retail investors, is the **index fund** (a pooled vehicle that seeks to replicate, rather than beat, the performance of a specified market index). The commercial case was proven, as noted in Chapter 1, by John Bogle's launch of the First Index Investment Trust in August 1976. For two decades index investing was a fringe strategy; by the early 2020s it had become the dominant form of equity investing in the United States. According to the Investment Company Institute's 2024 Fact Book, index mutual funds and index ETFs together held roughly 48 percent of long-term fund assets at the end of 2023, and index equity funds accounted for 57 percent of all equity fund assets by October 2024, up from 20 percent in 2012.[^6]

The reason for that shift is simple: cost. Every dollar paid in fund fees is a dollar deducted from the investor's return, every year, for as long as the position is held. The ICI reports that the asset-weighted average expense ratio on actively managed equity mutual funds in 2023 was 0.64 percent, while the average on index equity mutual funds was 0.05 percent.[^7] Compounded over a thirty-year holding period, a sixty-basis-point annual fee drag reduces terminal wealth by roughly 16 to 18 percent relative to a zero-fee benchmark. That arithmetic, more than any philosophical argument, is what has driven the migration of retail capital out of high-fee active products and into low-fee index products over the past two decades.

A modern retail investor can, for a total expense ratio of well under 10 basis points, assemble a globally diversified equity portfolio that would have been essentially unavailable at any price a generation ago. That democratization of access is, in many ways, the single most important development in equity investing since the end of fixed commissions.

## Modern Access Points

The path by which an individual actually owns a share of stock in 2026 bears little resemblance to the paper-certificate era that persisted well into the second half of the twentieth century. Three changes stand out. **Zero commissions**, as described in Chapter 1, became the industry standard for U.S. retail brokers in October 2019. **Fractional shares**, offered by every major discount broker, allow an investor to buy a dollar amount of a stock rather than a whole number of shares, meaning a $100 investment can purchase a proportional slice of a company whose share price is $500 or $5,000. **Mobile-first trading**, delivered through smartphone applications, has pushed account opening, funding, trading, and research into a single device. In 2024, Gallup reported that 62 percent of Americans owned stock in some form, a figure that rises to 87 percent for households earning $100,000 or more per year and falls to 28 percent for households earning less than $50,000.[^8] Participation is still unevenly distributed across the income spectrum, but the raw friction to getting started, once counted in weeks and hundreds of dollars, has collapsed to minutes and zero.

Robo-advisors, automated portfolio construction services that build and maintain low-cost, diversified portfolios based on a short investor questionnaire, are a fourth access point, treated in depth in Chapter 15. The point for this foundational chapter is that the infrastructure of equity investing has been flattened to a point where an investor of any experience level can establish a diversified position in the U.S. or global stock market for a few dollars and a few minutes of setup.

## Key Stock Metrics Introduced

Every asset class has a short list of quantitative metrics that recur in almost every analysis. For publicly traded equities, five are essential.

The **price-to-earnings ratio** (P/E ratio) is the current share price divided by the company's earnings per share over a trailing or forward twelve-month period. A P/E of 20 means an investor is paying twenty dollars for every dollar of annual earnings. The ratio is not comparable across sectors without adjustment, and it breaks down for companies with negative earnings, but it remains the default first glance at whether a stock is priced richly or modestly relative to its own history and its peers.

**Earnings per share** (EPS) is the company's net income divided by its average number of diluted shares outstanding. EPS is the numerator-side input to the P/E ratio and the measure against which Wall Street analyst estimates are most often expressed. Diluted EPS accounts for stock options, restricted stock units, and convertible securities that would increase the share count if exercised.

**Market capitalization**, defined earlier, is the simplest measure of company size and the basis for the capitalization weighting used by the S&P 500, the Russell 3000, the MSCI ACWI, and most other widely followed equity benchmarks. In a cap-weighted index, each constituent's contribution is proportional to its market capitalization, which is why the performance of the largest handful of U.S. technology companies has exerted an outsized influence on S&P 500 returns in recent years.

**Dividend yield**, also defined earlier, measures current income relative to price. Its complement, the **total yield**, adds to the dividend yield the percentage of shares the company repurchases in a given year, on the theory that a dollar returned through a buyback has the same economic effect as a dollar returned through a dividend.

**Beta** is a measure of a stock's price volatility relative to the broader market, typically the S&P 500. A beta of 1.0 implies the stock tends to move in line with the market, above 1.0 implies greater volatility, and below 1.0 implies lesser volatility. Beta is an input to the Capital Asset Pricing Model, examined in more depth in Chapter 14.

## Key Takeaways

- A common stock is a residual claim on a business, entitling the shareholder to a proportional share of earnings, assets, and governance, only after every senior claimant has been paid.
- Equity markets classify stocks by market capitalization, style (growth or value), geography, and GICS sector; these categories overlap, and a single share can belong to several at once.
- Dividends have historically accounted for a substantial share of long-run equity total returns, but they are declared at the discretion of the board of directors and can be cut or suspended without notice.
- U.S. listed stocks now trade primarily through electronic order books, settle on a T+1 cycle as of May 28, 2024, and can be transacted at zero commission through any major discount broker.
- Index mutual funds and index ETFs accounted for roughly 48 percent of long-term fund assets by year-end 2023 and 57 percent of equity fund assets by October 2024, a shift driven principally by the large and compounding cost advantage of passive over active management.
- The essential quantitative vocabulary of equity analysis, which recurs throughout the rest of the book, includes the P/E ratio, earnings per share, market capitalization, dividend yield, and beta.
