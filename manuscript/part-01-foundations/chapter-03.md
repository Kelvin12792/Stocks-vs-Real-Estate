## Introduction

Chapter 2 examined what it means to own a stock: a fractional, residual, legally defined claim on a business, held in abstract form and traded on an electronic exchange at near-zero friction. This chapter takes the opposite end of the spectrum. Real estate is concrete, physical, and local. A deed names a specific parcel of land, a specific structure on that land, and a specific owner responsible for its taxes, upkeep, and liabilities. Where a share of stock can be bought in a few seconds for a few dollars, a property typically takes weeks to purchase, tens of thousands of dollars to finance, and months or years to sell. The two asset classes differ not merely in their return profiles but in what it feels like to hold them.

For the purposes of Part I, however, the goal is not to compare. It is to describe. This chapter builds the second of the two self-contained portraits that Part I is responsible for constructing. It explains what real estate investing actually means, catalogues the forms the asset class takes, walks through the mechanics of a typical transaction, and introduces the core metrics, net operating income, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, and a handful of others, that the rest of the book will use without further definition. Chapter 4 will then place the language of stocks and the language of real estate side by side, and Part II will begin the comparison in earnest.

One point of terminology is worth establishing up front. Throughout this book, the phrase "real estate" refers to the asset class, not to any particular property. A family buying its first home is engaging with real estate in one sense, but it is doing so primarily as a consumer and a borrower rather than as an investor. A household buying a rental property, a share of a REIT, or an interest in a private real estate fund is engaging with real estate as an asset class. The distinction is sharper in Chapter 3 than in everyday conversation, where the two meanings are often used interchangeably, and sharper still in Chapter 7, where the economics of owner-occupied housing and income-producing property are examined side by side.

## What Real Estate Investing Actually Means

Real estate, at its most basic, is land and anything permanently attached to it. The legal term for this is **real property** (land together with all improvements, structures, and fixtures that are affixed to it and that transfer with it upon sale), distinguished from **personal property** (movable assets such as furniture, equipment, and inventory that do not transfer with the land unless explicitly included in the sale contract). When an investor buys a rental house, the land, the foundation, the framing, the roof, the heating and cooling systems, and the attached cabinetry are all part of the real property conveyed by the deed. A free-standing refrigerator may or may not be, depending on what the contract says. The line between real and personal property sounds academic but it is the first line any attorney or escrow officer draws when preparing a closing package.

Ownership of real property is recorded through a **deed** (a legal instrument that transfers title from a grantor to a grantee), which is filed with a county recorder's office and becomes part of the public record. The deed establishes who owns the property; it does not by itself establish that the title is free of competing claims. That is the job of a **title search** (a historical review of the public record to confirm that no outstanding liens, judgments, unpaid taxes, easements, or prior conveyances cloud the seller's ability to transfer clean ownership) and, in most transactions, of **title insurance** (a policy that indemnifies the new owner, the lender, or both against losses arising from defects in title that were not discovered at the time of the search). Stocks, by contrast, have no analog to title insurance. A share held in a brokerage account is recorded electronically through the Depository Trust Company, and the broker's internal books are the source of truth for ownership. Real estate carries its entire ownership history forward with it, and the paperwork reflects that.

The second thing worth establishing is the distinction between buying a home to live in and investing in real estate for returns. Most Americans' first and largest experience of real estate is the purchase of a primary residence, and the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances confirms that the primary residence remains the single largest asset category on the U.S. household balance sheet.[^1] Economically, however, a primary residence is a hybrid good. It provides a stream of housing services, the shelter, comfort, and convenience of not paying rent, that have imputed value to the owner even though no cash ever changes hands. It may also appreciate in market value over time. And it may be financed with a mortgage that amplifies both the equity gain on the way up and the loss on the way down. An investment property, by contrast, is held not for the housing services it provides to the owner but for the cash flows and capital appreciation it delivers. The two uses share a legal and financial infrastructure but differ sharply in their tax treatment, their accounting, and the way their returns are measured. Chapter 7 returns to the distinction in detail.

The third thing worth establishing is that real estate is, with rare exceptions, a **local asset** (one whose value, demand, operating costs, and regulatory environment are determined primarily by conditions in a specific neighborhood, city, or metropolitan area rather than by national aggregates). A rental property in Austin is, for almost every purpose that matters to its owner, a different investment from an otherwise identical rental property in Cleveland. Population trends, wage growth, zoning rules, property tax regimes, landlord-tenant law, insurance costs, and construction labor supply all vary meaningfully across markets, and even within a single metropolitan area they can vary from one zip code to the next. The S&P 500 is the S&P 500 everywhere it trades; a fourplex in one Texas suburb can outperform or underperform a fourplex ten miles away by a margin that no national statistic would capture. That locality is one of real estate's most distinctive features and one of the main reasons the asset class resists the kind of uniform summary that is routinely applied to stocks.

Taken together, these three points, the legal specificity of the asset, the difference between primary-residence use and investment use, and the local nature of value, frame the rest of the chapter. Real estate is not a portfolio ticker. It is a deed on a specific parcel in a specific market, held for a specific purpose, and the investor who treats it otherwise is likely to be surprised by it.

## Types of Real Estate Investments

The real estate asset class is typically divided into four broad property types, each with its own economics, tenant base, lease structures, and operating challenges. The categories are not airtight, and many investors and funds hold positions across more than one, but the divisions are widely used by lenders, appraisers, and industry analysts, and they set expectations for what a given property will demand of its owner.

**Residential** property is the category most familiar to individual investors. It includes **single-family rentals** (detached homes rented to a single household), **small multifamily** properties in the two-to-four unit range often called duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes, and **larger multifamily** apartment buildings of five units or more. The dividing line between small and large multifamily is not arbitrary: residential mortgages from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are generally available for properties of up to four units, while buildings of five units or more are financed through commercial multifamily loans underwritten against the property's income rather than the borrower's personal income. That financing distinction is one of the most consequential thresholds in the entire asset class, because it separates the owner-operator segment that most new investors enter from the institutional segment that large apartment owners inhabit.

**Commercial** property, used as an umbrella term, covers every income-producing real estate that is not residential. Industry convention further divides it into subtypes. **Office** property includes single-tenant and multi-tenant buildings leased to businesses, traditionally divided into Class A, Class B, and Class C tiers that reflect building quality, location, and tenant credit. **Retail** property includes neighborhood strip centers, grocery-anchored shopping centers, regional malls, and single-tenant net-leased stores. **Industrial** property includes warehouses, distribution centers, light manufacturing facilities, and cold storage. Each of these subtypes has its own tenant mix, its own lease length conventions, and its own vulnerability to economic cycles. Office, for example, has been under structural pressure since the rise of remote work in 2020; industrial has benefited from the buildout of e-commerce logistics over the same period.

**Specialty** property is a catch-all for asset types that do not fit cleanly into the residential or core commercial buckets. It includes hotels and hospitality, self-storage facilities, data centers, medical office buildings, senior housing, student housing, mobile home parks, and parking structures. Specialty categories can offer attractive economics but typically require more operational expertise than a passive investor can supply on their own, and they are often owned through specialized REITs or private funds rather than directly.

**Land** is the fourth category, and it is the outlier. Raw land, whether held as a speculation on future development, as a farm, or as a timber tract, produces little or no income while generating property tax liability and, depending on location, carrying costs for insurance, access, and environmental compliance. Land returns are almost entirely appreciation-based and are generally realized only on sale. Chapter 7 discusses in more detail why land is rarely suitable as a cash-flow investment for an individual.

A brief word on leases is warranted here because lease structure is the most important variable in distinguishing one real estate category from another. Residential leases are typically short-term, commonly one year, with the landlord bearing property taxes, insurance, and most maintenance. Commercial leases are typically longer, often three to ten years or more, and often structured as **triple-net leases** (leases in which the tenant is responsible for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to base rent), shifting a significant portion of operating risk from the owner to the occupant. The difference matters because two buildings with identical asking rents can deliver radically different cash flows depending on which party pays the expenses, and any serious analysis of a commercial property begins with understanding the lease before it touches the price.

## Indirect Real Estate Exposure

Direct ownership is only one way to gain exposure to the real estate asset class. A substantial and growing share of household real estate exposure flows through pooled vehicles that resemble, in many ways, the mutual funds and ETFs examined in Chapter 2. These indirect vehicles matter for two reasons. First, they make real estate accessible to investors who lack the capital, time, or appetite for direct ownership. Second, and equally important, they create the market-traded price signals that allow real estate to be compared with stocks at all. Without REITs, much of Part II of this book would have no apples-to-apples data to work with.

The most important indirect vehicle is the **real estate investment trust** (REIT), a corporation, trust, or association that owns and, in most cases, operates income-producing real estate, and that qualifies under Internal Revenue Code sections 856 through 860 for special tax treatment. The REIT regime, enacted by Public Law 86-779 and signed into law on September 14, 1960, was designed to give ordinary investors the ability to pool their capital into professionally managed real estate portfolios in much the same way that mutual funds let them pool capital into stocks.[^2] To qualify as a REIT, an entity must satisfy several structural tests, including that at least 75% of its total assets be invested in real estate, cash, and government securities; that at least 75% of its gross income come from real estate-related sources; that it have at least 100 shareholders; and, most famously, that it distribute at least **90% of its taxable income** to shareholders each year in the form of dividends.[^3] The 90% distribution requirement is the key economic feature of the structure: a REIT is, by design, a flow-through vehicle that pays out almost everything it earns and retains very little for internal reinvestment.

REITs divide into two major categories. **Equity REITs**, which own and operate physical real estate, are by far the larger category and are the ones most commonly discussed in the financial press. **Mortgage REITs**, often abbreviated mREITs, do not own real estate directly; they hold mortgage loans and mortgage-backed securities and earn the spread between their cost of capital and the yield on the loans they hold. The two categories behave very differently. Equity REITs derive their cash flows from rents and their total returns from a combination of rental income and appreciation in property values; mortgage REITs derive their cash flows from interest income and are, in effect, levered bets on interest-rate spreads. For the purposes of this book, unqualified references to "REITs" refer to equity REITs.

The size of the public REIT market is substantial. According to the National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts (Nareit), the U.S. listed equity REIT universe at the end of 2024 comprised roughly 150 REITs with an aggregate equity market capitalization in excess of $1.3 trillion, spread across residential, retail, office, industrial, health care, self-storage, data center, and specialty categories.[^4] That capitalization makes the listed REIT market comparable in size to several large single-country equity markets and large enough to support broad-market indexes such as the FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs Index, the MSCI US REIT Index, and the S&P United States REIT Index. It is this infrastructure that made possible the 2016 GICS reclassification noted in Chapter 2, which elevated real estate from a subsector of financials to a standalone sector.

Beyond listed REITs, several other indirect vehicles deserve brief mention. **Non-traded REITs**, which are registered with the SEC but do not trade on a public exchange, provide access to institutional-quality real estate but typically charge higher fees and impose lockups and redemption restrictions that can make exit difficult. **Real estate mutual funds and ETFs** hold diversified baskets of listed REITs and offer the same fee and diversification advantages that passive vehicles confer in the broader equity markets. **Real estate crowdfunding platforms**, enabled by Title III of the JOBS Act of 2012 and subsequent SEC rulemaking, allow accredited and, in some cases, non-accredited investors to take direct fractional positions in individual properties or pools of properties. **Private syndications**, typically organized as limited partnerships or limited liability companies and sold under Regulation D of the Securities Act, remain the traditional vehicle through which high-net-worth investors co-invest alongside experienced sponsors in individual deals. Chapter 12 returns to crowdfunding and syndications in its discussion of barriers to entry. For the foundational purposes of this chapter, the point is simply that the real estate asset class is no longer accessible only through direct purchase, and the indirect channels are an increasingly important share of the overall market.

## How Real Estate Transactions Work

Buying a stock is a single click. Buying a property is, even in the best-case scenario, a months-long process involving a contract, a lender, an inspector, an appraiser, a title company, an insurer, and a closing agent, and generating a stack of paperwork that typically runs to several hundred pages. Each step exists to protect one party or another from a category of risk that, in the equity markets, does not exist or is handled invisibly by intermediaries. Understanding the sequence is essential, because the transaction costs and timelines it implies are among the most important differences between the two asset classes.

A residential real estate transaction in the United States typically begins when a buyer, often working through a licensed **real estate agent** (a professional licensed by the state to represent a buyer or seller in a real estate transaction), identifies a property and submits a written **purchase offer**. The offer specifies the price, the financing contingency, the inspection contingency, the proposed closing date, the earnest money deposit, and any other conditions. Negotiation proceeds in writing until both sides sign an agreement, at which point the property goes **under contract** and the clock starts on a series of contingency periods that the buyer must work through to reach closing.

**Due diligence** is the collective name for the buyer-side work performed during those contingency periods. A **home inspection** is typically the first step, in which a licensed inspector walks the property and produces a report on its structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing condition. A **property appraisal**, ordered by the lender on behalf of the mortgage program, is the second, in which a licensed appraiser estimates the property's market value by comparing it with recent sales of similar properties in the same area. The appraisal exists to protect the lender from over-lending against a property that is not worth the purchase price; if the appraisal comes in below the contract price, the buyer must either renegotiate, make up the shortfall in cash, or walk away. A **title search** and, in most cases, a **survey** of the property boundaries are performed in parallel, and any defects uncovered during due diligence can trigger renegotiation or termination of the contract.

Financing runs in parallel with due diligence. A buyer who is paying cash skips this track entirely, but the large majority of residential transactions involve a mortgage. The **mortgage underwriting** process evaluates the borrower's credit, income, assets, and employment, and it evaluates the property itself as collateral. Underwriting routinely takes thirty to sixty days and culminates in a **clear to close** notice from the lender, at which point final closing documents can be prepared. Throughout the underwriting period, the buyer is typically barred from opening new credit accounts, changing jobs, or making large deposits of unexplained cash, any of which can trigger a re-underwrite and delay closing.

**Closing** is the event at which ownership formally changes hands. The buyer signs a stack of loan and disclosure documents, the lender wires the loan proceeds to the closing agent, the buyer wires the remaining cash due, and the closing agent disburses the funds to the seller, the existing lender (to pay off any prior mortgage), the real estate agents (to pay commissions), the title company, the insurer, and any other parties with a claim against the transaction. The **settlement statement**, sometimes called a Closing Disclosure on residential purchases, itemizes every charge. Once the deed is signed by the seller and recorded by the county, the buyer is the legal owner of the property. The whole process, from accepted offer to recorded deed, typically takes between thirty and sixty days on a conventional mortgage.

The transaction costs embedded in that process are substantial and recurring. On the sell side, the traditional U.S. model has been for the seller to pay a commission of roughly 5 to 6 percent of the sale price, split between the listing broker and the buyer's broker. Following the National Association of Realtors antitrust settlement that took effect in August 2024, buyer-broker compensation is no longer advertised through the MLS and is instead negotiated directly between the buyer and the buyer's broker, a change that is reshaping commission practice across the country without, so far, producing a uniform new norm.[^5] On the buy side, closing costs including lender fees, appraisal fees, title insurance, recording fees, prepaid property taxes, and prepaid insurance typically run 2 to 5 percent of the purchase price in a conventional residential transaction.[^6] The combined round-trip cost of buying and then selling a single property, ignoring any capital improvements made in between, is therefore commonly on the order of 7 to 11 percent of the property's value. That frictional cost is one of the strongest arguments in favor of long holding periods for direct real estate, and it is a cost that has no real counterpart on the equity side of the ledger, where a round-trip trade in an S&P 500 ETF can be executed for a fraction of a basis point.

## Property Management

Owning a property is not the same as operating one. Once a rental is acquired, it must be marketed when vacant, screened for tenants, leased, maintained, repaired, insured, inspected, and financially reconciled on an ongoing basis. The rent must be collected, the utilities and taxes must be paid on time, and the books must be kept in a form that will survive scrutiny from a tax preparer and, eventually, from the IRS. For the owner of a single small property, these duties can be handled personally in a few hours a week; for the owner of a ten-unit building, they are a part-time job; for the owner of a fifty-unit portfolio, they are a full-time occupation or an outsourced function.

**Self-management** means the owner performs these duties directly. The advantage is cost: the owner keeps the management fee that would otherwise be paid to a third party, which can materially improve cash flow on a small portfolio. The disadvantage is time, and, more importantly, availability. A tenant's broken water heater at 11 p.m. on a Saturday is not a problem that can be deferred until Monday morning, and the self-manager is the first call. Self-managers must also stay current on local landlord-tenant law, which governs everything from permissible security deposit amounts to eviction procedures and varies sharply from one jurisdiction to the next.

**Professional property management**, the alternative, is performed by a company that takes on the day-to-day operation of the property in exchange for a fee. The standard U.S. residential management fee structure has two components: a **monthly management fee** of typically 8 to 12 percent of collected rents, and a **leasing fee** of typically half a month to a full month's rent, charged when a new tenant is placed.[^7] On top of these, property managers may bill a separate lease renewal fee, a maintenance coordination fee, an eviction fee, and markups on contractor invoices. An investor evaluating a property must therefore look beyond the headline management percentage and understand the full set of charges that the management contract authorizes. In commercial real estate, management fee structures are often quoted as a percentage of gross income, and they are frequently lower than residential rates because commercial tenants bear more of their own operating responsibilities under triple-net leases.

The decision between self-management and professional management is often framed as a question of cost, but it is more usefully framed as a question of the owner's time and the owner's comparative advantage. An investor whose day job pays a higher effective hourly rate than the property management fee saves is, on purely economic grounds, better served by hiring out the work. An investor whose property is far from where they live has little realistic alternative to hiring out the work. An investor who finds tenant relations stressful or who lacks the temperament to handle difficult conversations has a non-financial reason to do so. The important point for the purposes of Part I is that ownership and operation are separable, that operation has a real cost in either money or time or both, and that the headline return figures published by real estate industry groups often implicitly assume that management cost is accounted for, even when individual investors underwriting a deal forget to subtract it.

## Key Real Estate Metrics Introduced

Just as equity analysis has a short list of standard metrics, real estate analysis has its own. The metrics introduced here are the ones that will recur throughout Parts II, III, and IV without further definition. Each is defined briefly, and each has an intuitive counterpart somewhere in the vocabulary of stocks, a parallel that Chapter 4 will draw out explicitly.

**Net operating income** (NOI) is the property's gross rental income minus its operating expenses, before financing costs and before income taxes. Operating expenses include property taxes, insurance, maintenance, repairs, property management fees, utilities paid by the owner, and a vacancy and bad-debt allowance. NOI is the real estate analog to a company's operating income, and it is the single most important income measure in commercial real estate analysis. Notably, NOI excludes mortgage interest, principal amortization, depreciation, and income taxes, which are treated separately because they depend on how the property is financed and owned rather than on how the property itself operates.

**Capitalization rate**, almost always shortened to **cap rate**, is the property's NOI divided by its purchase price or current market value, expressed as a percentage. A cap rate of 6 percent means that, before financing and taxes, the property is generating annual income equal to 6 percent of its value. Cap rates are the real estate market's equivalent of an earnings yield. They move inversely with price: when cap rates fall, it typically means buyers are paying more per dollar of income, the way a rising stock market compresses earnings yields. Cap rates vary by property type, by location, and by perceived risk, and a property's cap rate at the time of purchase is often the strongest single predictor of its long-run cash yield.

**Cash-on-cash return** is the pre-tax cash flow from the property in a given year divided by the actual cash the investor has invested in it. If a property generates $8,000 of cash flow after mortgage payments and the investor put $100,000 into the deal, the cash-on-cash return for that year is 8 percent. Unlike the cap rate, cash-on-cash return accounts for financing, which is both its strength and its limitation: it reflects what the investor actually earns on invested equity, but it conflates operating performance with the terms of the loan, and it can be inflated to meaningless levels by extreme leverage.

**Gross rent multiplier** (GRM) is the purchase price divided by the annual gross rent. It is a rougher cousin of the cap rate, widely used as a quick screening tool on residential deals where operating expenses are not yet fully known. A GRM of 10 means the property is priced at ten times its annual gross rent. GRM is useful for initial comparisons and is useless for final decisions, because two properties with the same GRM can have very different NOI depending on how expenses break down.

**Vacancy rate** is the share of a property's total rentable units or square footage that is unoccupied at a given time, expressed as a percentage. It can be measured on a specific date, over a trailing twelve months, or as a market-level average compiled by firms such as CoStar or Yardi for commercial property and by the U.S. Census Bureau for residential property. A property that underwrites at a 5 percent vacancy assumption is one that is expected to be vacant, on average, roughly one month out of every twenty. Vacancy interacts directly with every other return metric, because every dollar of lost rent is a dollar subtracted from NOI, from cap rate, and from cash-on-cash return.

**Appreciation rate** is the annualized change in the property's market value over time. It is the real estate counterpart to price return on a stock, and like price return it is only one component of total return. The other component is rental cash flow, which, over long holding periods, typically accounts for the majority of the property's all-in return. An investor who focuses solely on appreciation is the real estate equivalent of a stock investor who ignores dividends and buybacks.

The five metrics above, NOI, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, GRM, and vacancy rate, plus the concept of appreciation, are the vocabulary of a single property's economics. Chapter 4 adds a handful of portfolio-level metrics, and Chapter 7 uses them to compare the cash flow profiles of real estate and equity investments.

## The Role of the Mortgage

The single most important feature that distinguishes real estate investing from stock investing, for most individual investors, is not the asset itself but the financing available against it. Residential real estate in the United States can be financed with thirty-year fixed-rate **mortgage loans** at loan-to-value ratios of up to 80 percent on investment properties and up to 97 percent on owner-occupied homes, with interest rates subsidized through a network of federal programs and guarantees that have no counterpart in the equity markets. A small investor who wants to buy $500,000 of publicly traded stock on margin would, under Federal Reserve Regulation T, be allowed to borrow no more than $250,000 at current broker call rates; the same investor who wants to buy a $500,000 house as a primary residence can typically borrow $400,000 or more at a thirty-year fixed rate that may be substantially lower than the margin rate, and can do so with no margin call if the house's market value falls.

The implications are enormous. **Leverage** (the use of borrowed capital to increase the potential return on invested equity) amplifies gains and losses on the equity portion of the investment. A property purchased for $500,000 with $100,000 down that appreciates 10 percent to $550,000 has produced a 50 percent return on the investor's equity, ignoring transaction costs and debt service. The same property declining 10 percent to $450,000 has produced a 50 percent loss on equity, ignoring the same items. The non-recourse nature of many residential mortgages in some states further changes the risk calculus, because the worst-case outcome for the borrower may be the loss of the property rather than a personal judgment for the remaining loan balance. Chapter 11 is dedicated to a full treatment of leverage, and it returns to this calculation in far more depth.

For the purposes of Chapter 3, the relevant point is simpler: the mortgage is not an ancillary detail of real estate investing. For most individual investors, it is the single feature that most distinguishes the asset class from stocks, and any portrait of real estate that ignores financing is incomplete. The availability of long-duration, fixed-rate, high-LTV financing is a gift of twentieth-century federal housing policy, traced in Chapter 1, and it is the reason direct real estate investing is economically accessible to households that would never dream of borrowing four-fifths of the cost of a stock portfolio. It is also the reason real estate can produce returns that appear competitive with stocks on a cash-on-cash basis even when its unlevered returns are lower. The arithmetic of leverage is doing much of the work, and Chapter 11 will make that arithmetic explicit.

## Key Takeaways

- Real estate is a legal claim on a specific parcel of land and its permanent improvements, and the local nature of that claim makes it resistant to the kind of national-aggregate generalization routinely applied to stocks.
- The asset class divides into residential, commercial, specialty, and land categories, each with distinct lease conventions, financing thresholds, and operating demands, and the dividing line between small multifamily and larger multifamily is one of the most consequential in direct real estate investing.
- Indirect real estate exposure through REITs, non-traded REITs, real estate mutual funds and ETFs, crowdfunding platforms, and private syndications has broadened access to the asset class and made stock-to-real-estate comparison statistically possible.
- A real estate transaction is a months-long, multi-party process that generates round-trip transaction costs of roughly 7 to 11 percent of property value, a frictional cost that has no real counterpart on the equity side of the ledger.
- The core single-property metrics, net operating income, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, gross rent multiplier, vacancy rate, and appreciation, together describe how a property performs and will be used without further definition throughout the rest of the book.
- The single most important structural difference between real estate and stocks, for most individual investors, is the availability of long-duration, high-LTV, fixed-rate mortgage financing, a feature that amplifies both returns and risks and that has no equivalent in the equity markets.
