## Introduction

Chapter 2 examined what it means to own a stock: a fractional, legally defined claim on a business, held in abstract form and traded on an electronic exchange at near-zero friction. This chapter takes the opposite end of the spectrum. Real estate is concrete, physical, and local. A deed names a specific parcel of land, a specific structure, and a specific owner responsible for its taxes, upkeep, and liabilities. Where a share of stock can be bought in seconds for a few dollars, a property typically takes weeks to purchase and months or years to sell.

The goal in Part I is not to compare but to describe. This chapter explains what real estate investing actually means, catalogues the forms the asset class takes, walks through the mechanics of a typical transaction, and introduces the core metrics, net operating income, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, and others, that the rest of the book will use without further definition.

One point of terminology is worth establishing up front. Throughout this book, "real estate" refers to the asset class. A family buying its first home is engaging with real estate primarily as a consumer and a borrower; a household buying a rental property, a share of a REIT, or an interest in a private real estate fund is engaging with it as an asset class. Chapter 7 returns to this distinction in detail.

## What Real Estate Investing Actually Means

Real estate, at its most basic, is land and anything permanently attached to it. The legal term for this is **real property** (land together with all improvements, structures, and fixtures that transfer with it upon sale), distinguished from **personal property** (movable assets that do not transfer with the land unless explicitly included in the sale contract). When an investor buys a rental house, the land, the framing, the roof, the heating and cooling systems, and the attached cabinetry are part of the real property conveyed by the deed; a free-standing refrigerator may or may not be, depending on what the contract says.

Ownership is recorded through a **deed** (a legal instrument that transfers title from a grantor to a grantee), filed with a county recorder's office. The deed establishes who owns the property; it does not establish that the title is free of competing claims. That is the job of a **title search** (a historical review of the public record to confirm no outstanding liens, judgments, unpaid taxes, easements, or prior conveyances cloud the seller's ability to transfer clean ownership) and, in most transactions, of **title insurance** (a policy that indemnifies the new owner and the lender against undiscovered title defects). Stocks have no analog to title insurance: a share is recorded electronically through the Depository Trust Company and the broker's internal books are the source of truth. Real estate carries its entire ownership history forward with it, and the paperwork reflects that.

The second point worth establishing is the distinction between buying a home to live in and investing in real estate for returns. Most Americans' first and largest experience of real estate is the purchase of a primary residence, and the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances confirms that the primary residence remains the single largest asset category on the U.S. household balance sheet.[^1] A primary residence is a hybrid good: it provides a stream of housing services whose imputed value accrues to the owner, and it may appreciate in market value. An investment property, by contrast, is held for the cash flows and capital appreciation it delivers. The two uses share a legal and financial infrastructure but differ sharply in their tax treatment, their accounting, and the way their returns are measured.

The third point is that real estate is, with rare exceptions, a **local asset** (one whose value, demand, operating costs, and regulatory environment are determined primarily by conditions in a specific neighborhood or metropolitan area rather than by national aggregates). Population trends, wage growth, zoning rules, property tax regimes, landlord-tenant law, and insurance costs all vary meaningfully across markets, and even within a metropolitan area they can vary from one zip code to the next. The S&P 500 is the S&P 500 everywhere it trades; a fourplex in one suburb can outperform or underperform a fourplex ten miles away by a margin no national statistic would capture. That locality is one of real estate's most distinctive features and a main reason the asset class resists the kind of uniform summary routinely applied to stocks.

## Types of Real Estate Investments

The real estate asset class is typically divided into four broad property types, each with its own economics, tenant base, lease structures, and operating challenges.

**Residential** property is the category most familiar to individual investors. It includes **single-family rentals** (detached homes rented to a single household), **small multifamily** properties in the two-to-four unit range (duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes), and **larger multifamily** apartment buildings of five units or more. The dividing line is not arbitrary: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac residential mortgages are available for properties of up to four units, while buildings of five units or more are financed through commercial multifamily loans underwritten against the property's income rather than the borrower's personal income. That threshold separates the owner-operator segment that most new investors enter from the institutional segment that large apartment owners inhabit.

**Commercial** property covers every income-producing real estate that is not residential. **Office** buildings are traditionally divided into Class A, Class B, and Class C tiers reflecting building quality, location, and tenant credit. **Retail** property includes neighborhood strip centers, grocery-anchored centers, regional malls, and single-tenant net-leased stores. **Industrial** property includes warehouses, distribution centers, light manufacturing, and cold storage. Each subtype has its own lease length conventions and vulnerability to economic cycles. Office has been under structural pressure since the rise of remote work in 2020; industrial has benefited from the buildout of e-commerce logistics over the same period.

**Specialty** property is a catch-all for asset types that do not fit cleanly into the residential or core commercial buckets: hotels, self-storage, data centers, medical office, senior housing, student housing, mobile home parks, and parking structures. Specialty categories can offer attractive economics but typically require operational expertise beyond what a passive investor can supply directly, and they are often owned through specialized REITs or private funds.

**Land** is the fourth category and the outlier. Raw land produces little or no income while generating property tax liability and carrying costs. Land returns are almost entirely appreciation-based and realized only on sale. Chapter 7 discusses why land is rarely suitable as a cash-flow investment for an individual.

Lease structure is the most important variable in distinguishing one real estate category from another. Residential leases are typically short-term (commonly one year), with the landlord bearing property taxes, insurance, and most maintenance. Commercial leases are longer, often three to ten years or more, and frequently structured as **triple-net leases** (leases in which the tenant is responsible for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to base rent), shifting operating risk from owner to occupant. Two buildings with identical asking rents can deliver radically different cash flows depending on which party pays the expenses, and any serious analysis of a commercial property begins with the lease before it touches the price.

## Indirect Real Estate Exposure

Direct ownership is only one way to gain exposure to real estate. A growing share of household real estate exposure flows through pooled vehicles that resemble the mutual funds and ETFs examined in Chapter 2. These vehicles make the asset class accessible to investors who lack the capital, time, or appetite for direct ownership, and they create the market-traded price signals that allow real estate to be compared with stocks at all.

The most important indirect vehicle is the **real estate investment trust** (REIT), a corporation, trust, or association that owns and, in most cases, operates income-producing real estate, and that qualifies under Internal Revenue Code sections 856 through 860 for special tax treatment. The REIT regime, enacted by Public Law 86-779 and signed into law on September 14, 1960, was designed to give ordinary investors the ability to pool capital into professionally managed real estate portfolios in the same way that mutual funds let them pool capital into stocks.[^2] To qualify as a REIT, an entity must satisfy several structural tests, including that at least 75% of its assets be invested in real estate, cash, and government securities; that at least 75% of its gross income come from real estate-related sources; and, most famously, that it distribute at least **90% of its taxable income** to shareholders each year in the form of dividends.[^3] The 90% distribution requirement is the key economic feature: a REIT is, by design, a flow-through vehicle that pays out almost everything it earns.

REITs divide into two major categories. **Equity REITs** own and operate physical real estate; they derive cash flows from rents and total returns from a combination of rental income and property appreciation. **Mortgage REITs** (mREITs) hold mortgage loans and mortgage-backed securities and earn the spread between their cost of capital and their loan yields, behaving in effect as levered bets on interest-rate spreads. For the purposes of this book, unqualified references to "REITs" refer to equity REITs.

According to the National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts (Nareit), the U.S. listed equity REIT universe at the end of 2024 comprised roughly 150 REITs with an aggregate equity market capitalization in excess of $1.3 trillion, spread across residential, retail, office, industrial, health care, self-storage, data center, and specialty categories.[^4] That capitalization is large enough to support broad-market indexes such as the FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs Index and the MSCI US REIT Index, and it is this infrastructure that made possible the 2016 GICS reclassification noted in Chapter 2, which elevated real estate from a subsector of financials to a standalone sector.

Beyond listed REITs, several other vehicles deserve mention. **Non-traded REITs** are registered with the SEC but do not trade on a public exchange, typically charging higher fees and imposing lockups. **Real estate mutual funds and ETFs** hold diversified baskets of listed REITs. **Real estate crowdfunding platforms**, enabled by Title III of the JOBS Act of 2012, allow accredited and, in some cases, non-accredited investors to take fractional positions in individual properties. **Private syndications**, sold under Regulation D of the Securities Act, remain the traditional vehicle for high-net-worth investors co-investing alongside sponsors in individual deals. Chapter 12 returns to crowdfunding and syndications in its discussion of barriers to entry.

## How Real Estate Transactions Work

Buying a stock is a single click. Buying a property is a months-long process involving a contract, a lender, an inspector, an appraiser, a title company, an insurer, and a closing agent, generating paperwork that runs to several hundred pages. Each step exists to protect one party from a category of risk that, in the equity markets, does not exist or is handled invisibly by intermediaries.

A residential transaction typically begins when a buyer, often working through a licensed **real estate agent** (a professional licensed by the state to represent a buyer or seller in a transaction), submits a written **purchase offer** specifying price, financing and inspection contingencies, proposed closing date, and earnest money deposit. Once both sides sign, the property goes **under contract** and a series of contingency periods begin.

**Due diligence** is the collective name for the buyer-side work performed during those periods. A **home inspection** produces a report on the property's structural, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing condition. A **property appraisal**, ordered by the lender, estimates market value by comparing recent sales of similar properties; if the appraisal comes in below contract price, the buyer must renegotiate, cover the shortfall in cash, or walk away. A **title search** and, in most cases, a **survey** of the boundaries run in parallel, and any defects uncovered can trigger renegotiation or termination.

Financing runs alongside due diligence. A cash buyer skips this track, but most residential deals involve a mortgage. **Mortgage underwriting** evaluates the borrower's credit, income, assets, and employment, and the property itself as collateral. Underwriting routinely takes thirty to sixty days and culminates in a **clear to close** notice. Throughout the period, the buyer is typically barred from opening new credit, changing jobs, or making large unexplained deposits, any of which can trigger a re-underwrite.

**Closing** is the event at which ownership formally changes hands. The buyer signs loan and disclosure documents, the lender wires loan proceeds, the buyer wires remaining cash, and the closing agent disburses funds to the seller, any prior lender, the real estate agents, the title company, and the insurer. The **settlement statement**, called a Closing Disclosure on residential purchases, itemizes every charge. Once the deed is recorded by the county, the buyer is the legal owner. The full process, from accepted offer to recorded deed, typically takes thirty to sixty days on a conventional mortgage.

The transaction costs embedded in that process are substantial. On the sell side, the traditional U.S. model has been for the seller to pay a commission of roughly 5 to 6 percent of the sale price, split between listing and buyer brokers. Following the National Association of Realtors antitrust settlement effective August 17, 2024, buyer-broker compensation is no longer advertised through the MLS and is instead negotiated directly between buyer and buyer's broker, a change now reshaping commission practice without yet producing a uniform new norm.[^5] On the buy side, closing costs, lender fees, appraisal, title insurance, recording fees, and prepaid taxes and insurance, typically run 2 to 5 percent of the purchase price.[^6] Round-trip cost of buying and then selling a single property is therefore commonly on the order of 7 to 11 percent of value, a frictional cost with no counterpart on the equity side, where a round-trip in an S&P 500 ETF costs a fraction of a basis point.

## Property Management

Owning a property is not the same as operating one. A rental must be marketed when vacant, screened for tenants, leased, maintained, repaired, insured, inspected, and financially reconciled on an ongoing basis. Rent must be collected, utilities and taxes paid on time, and books kept in a form that will survive scrutiny from a tax preparer and the IRS. For the owner of a single small property these duties can be handled personally in a few hours a week; for a ten-unit building they are a part-time job; for a fifty-unit portfolio they are a full-time occupation or an outsourced function.

**Self-management** means the owner performs these duties directly. The advantage is cost, because the owner keeps the fee that would otherwise go to a third party. The disadvantage is time, and more importantly availability: a tenant's broken water heater at 11 p.m. on a Saturday cannot be deferred until Monday morning. Self-managers must also stay current on local landlord-tenant law, which governs everything from permissible security deposits to eviction procedures and varies sharply between jurisdictions.

**Professional property management** is performed by a company that operates the property in exchange for a fee. The standard U.S. residential structure has two components: a **monthly management fee** of typically 8 to 12 percent of collected rents, and a **leasing fee** of typically half a month to a full month's rent, charged when a new tenant is placed.[^7] On top of these, managers may bill a lease renewal fee, a maintenance coordination fee, an eviction fee, and markups on contractor invoices. An investor must look beyond the headline percentage to the full set of charges the contract authorizes. In commercial real estate, management fees are often quoted as a percentage of gross income and are frequently lower than residential rates because triple-net tenants bear more operating responsibility themselves.

The decision between self and professional management is often framed as cost but is more usefully framed as a question of the owner's time and comparative advantage. An investor whose day job pays more per hour than professional management saves is, on purely economic grounds, better served by hiring out the work. The important point for Part I is that ownership and operation are separable, that operation has a real cost in money, time, or both, and that headline return figures often implicitly assume management cost is accounted for even when individual investors underwriting a deal forget to subtract it.

## Key Real Estate Metrics Introduced

Just as equity analysis has a short list of standard metrics, so does real estate. The ones introduced here will recur throughout Parts II, III, and IV without further definition, and each has a counterpart somewhere in the vocabulary of stocks that Chapter 4 will draw out explicitly.

**Net operating income** (NOI) is the property's gross rental income minus its operating expenses, before financing costs and income taxes. Operating expenses include property taxes, insurance, maintenance, repairs, management fees, owner-paid utilities, and a vacancy and bad-debt allowance. NOI is the real estate analog to a company's operating income and is the single most important income measure in commercial analysis. It excludes mortgage interest, principal amortization, depreciation, and income taxes, which depend on how the property is financed and owned rather than on how it operates.

**Capitalization rate**, almost always shortened to **cap rate**, is NOI divided by purchase price or market value, expressed as a percentage. A cap rate of 6 percent means the property generates annual income equal to 6 percent of its value before financing and taxes. Cap rates are the real estate market's equivalent of an earnings yield; they move inversely with price, the way a rising stock market compresses earnings yields. A property's entry cap rate is often the strongest single predictor of its long-run cash yield.

**Cash-on-cash return** is the pre-tax annual cash flow divided by the actual cash the investor has invested. If a property generates $8,000 of cash flow after debt service and the investor put $100,000 down, the cash-on-cash return is 8 percent. Unlike the cap rate, it accounts for financing, which is both its strength and its limitation: it reflects what the investor actually earns on equity but conflates operating performance with loan terms and can be inflated to meaningless levels by extreme leverage.

**Gross rent multiplier** (GRM) is the purchase price divided by annual gross rent. A GRM of 10 means the property is priced at ten times gross rent. It is a quick screening tool on residential deals where operating expenses are not yet known, but useless for final decisions, because two properties with the same GRM can have very different NOI.

**Vacancy rate** is the share of a property's rentable units or square footage that is unoccupied, expressed as a percentage. A 5 percent vacancy assumption implies vacancy roughly one month out of every twenty. Vacancy interacts directly with every other return metric, because every dollar of lost rent is a dollar subtracted from NOI, cap rate, and cash-on-cash return.

**Appreciation rate** is the annualized change in the property's market value. It is the counterpart to price return on a stock, and like price return it is only one component of total return. The other component is rental cash flow, which over long holding periods typically accounts for the majority of all-in return. An investor who focuses solely on appreciation is the real estate equivalent of a stock investor who ignores dividends and buybacks.

These metrics, NOI, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, GRM, vacancy rate, and appreciation, are the vocabulary of a single property's economics. Chapter 4 adds portfolio-level metrics, and Chapter 7 uses them to compare the cash flow profiles of real estate and equity investments.

## The Role of the Mortgage

The single most important feature distinguishing real estate investing from stock investing, for most individual investors, is not the asset itself but the financing available against it. Residential real estate in the United States can be financed with thirty-year fixed-rate **mortgage loans** at loan-to-value ratios of up to 80 percent on investment properties and up to 97 percent on owner-occupied homes, on terms shaped by a network of federal programs and guarantees that have no counterpart in the equity markets. An investor who wants to buy $500,000 of publicly traded stock on margin would, under Federal Reserve Regulation T, be allowed to borrow no more than $250,000 at broker call rates; the same investor who wants to buy a $500,000 primary residence can typically borrow $400,000 or more at a thirty-year fixed rate, often lower than the margin rate, with no margin call if the house's value falls.

**Leverage** (the use of borrowed capital to increase the potential return on invested equity) amplifies gains and losses on the equity portion of an investment. A property bought for $500,000 with $100,000 down that appreciates 10 percent to $550,000 produces a 50 percent return on equity, ignoring transaction costs and debt service. The same property declining 10 percent produces a 50 percent loss on the same basis. Chapter 11 is dedicated to a full treatment of leverage and returns to this arithmetic in depth.

For the purposes of Chapter 3, the point is simpler: the mortgage is not an ancillary detail of real estate investing. For most individual investors it is the feature that most distinguishes the asset class from stocks, and any portrait of real estate that ignores financing is incomplete. The availability of long-duration, fixed-rate, high-LTV financing is a gift of twentieth-century federal housing policy traced in Chapter 1, and it is the reason direct real estate is economically accessible to households that would never dream of borrowing four-fifths of the cost of a stock portfolio. It is also the reason real estate can produce cash-on-cash returns competitive with stocks even when its unlevered returns are lower. The arithmetic of leverage is doing much of the work.

## Key Takeaways

- Real estate is a legal claim on a specific parcel of land and its permanent improvements, and the local nature of that claim makes it resistant to the kind of national-aggregate generalization routinely applied to stocks.
- The asset class divides into residential, commercial, specialty, and land categories, each with distinct lease conventions, financing thresholds, and operating demands, and the dividing line between small multifamily and larger multifamily is one of the most consequential in direct real estate investing.
- Indirect real estate exposure through REITs, non-traded REITs, real estate mutual funds and ETFs, crowdfunding platforms, and private syndications has broadened access to the asset class and made stock-to-real-estate comparison statistically possible.
- A real estate transaction is a months-long, multi-party process that generates round-trip transaction costs of roughly 7 to 11 percent of property value, a frictional cost that has no real counterpart on the equity side of the ledger.
- The core single-property metrics, net operating income, cap rate, cash-on-cash return, gross rent multiplier, vacancy rate, and appreciation, together describe how a property performs and will be used without further definition throughout the rest of the book.
- The single most important structural difference between real estate and stocks, for most individual investors, is the availability of long-duration, high-LTV, fixed-rate mortgage financing, a feature that amplifies both returns and risks and that has no equivalent in the equity markets.
