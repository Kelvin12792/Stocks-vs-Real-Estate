## Introduction

Chapter 5 answered the question of where money has historically grown faster and showed that the answer depends heavily on leverage. This chapter takes up the counterpart question that any honest return comparison must also address: what could go wrong, and how badly? A 7 percent real annualized return is not the same asset if it arrives with a 10 percent maximum drawdown as if it arrives with a 55 percent drawdown, even when both series compound to the same terminal value. The path matters because the investor lives on the path, not at the endpoint, and the investor's decision to hold or sell in the middle of that path is the single largest determinant of whether the terminal value is actually captured.

Risk in investing is commonly reduced to a single number, the standard deviation of annual returns, and then the two asset classes are compared on that one dimension as if it were the whole story. It is not. Stocks and real estate have structurally different risk profiles that do not compress neatly into a single volatility figure. Stock market risk is priced continuously, published every second, and impossible to ignore; residential real estate risk is priced rarely, smoothed by appraisal, and much easier to look away from, which is not the same as being absent. Each asset class has categories of risk the other largely does not face, and each has categories of risk that look the same at first glance but operate by very different mechanisms underneath.

This chapter walks through the main categories one at a time. It begins by separating volatility from permanent loss of capital, two ideas that are often conflated. It then compares the two asset classes on market-level volatility using the standard long-run datasets already introduced, and explains why the reported volatility gap between stocks and housing is partly real and partly an artifact of how housing returns are measured. It turns to idiosyncratic risk and the different mechanics of diversification in each asset class. It revisits leverage, the single amplifier that turns either asset into a substantially riskier position, with a specific focus on the asymmetric downside introduced in Chapter 5. It covers liquidity and life-event risk, the category that most often converts a temporary market move into a permanent loss. It closes with a treatment of tail risk and black swan events, drawing on the 2008 and COVID case studies flagged in Chapter 1, before turning to the persona, Sarah Okonkwo, a 45-year-old Atlanta nurse whose question is the one that gives the chapter its shape: "I lived through 2008 and it scared me. What's actually safer, more real estate or more stocks?"

The honest summary is this: stocks are more visibly volatile than unleveraged residential real estate, but once leverage and concentration enter the picture, direct real estate can easily match or exceed stock volatility in the dimensions that actually matter to an individual owner. The asset class with the scary headlines is not always the one with the scarier tail, and the asset class with the quiet prices is not always the one with the smaller risk.

## What "Risk" Actually Means

In everyday speech, risk and volatility are used interchangeably. In investment analysis they are related but distinct, and the distinction is worth making explicit before any comparison begins. **Volatility** (the degree to which an investment's returns vary around their average, most commonly measured by the standard deviation of periodic returns) is a description of how bumpy the ride is. **Risk** (the probability and magnitude of an unfavorable outcome, especially a permanent loss of capital) is a description of what can go wrong and how badly. Volatility contributes to risk but is not the same thing. A perfectly volatile asset that always recovers is not risky in the permanent-loss sense; a quiet asset that slowly goes to zero is extremely risky even if its reported standard deviation is small.

The two concepts diverge most sharply around the idea of **drawdown** (the peak-to-trough decline in the value of an investment measured from its previous high, usually expressed as a percentage). Drawdown is what an investor actually experiences when they open their brokerage statement or appraise their rental property in a bad year. A 50 percent drawdown means the portfolio has lost half of its value from its previous peak, and recovering from that drawdown requires a subsequent 100 percent gain, not a 50 percent gain, because the gain is computed against the lower base. Drawdown statistics are therefore asymmetric in a way that simple volatility statistics are not, and they correspond more closely to the lived experience of risk than standard deviation does.

A third useful idea is **permanent loss of capital** (the portion of a drawdown that is never recovered, either because the investor is forced to sell at the bottom, because the asset itself never recovers, or because the investor's time horizon expires before the recovery arrives). Permanent loss is the kind of risk that actually damages an investment plan. A temporary 40 percent decline that is held through and recovered is, in the long run, a volatility event; the same 40 percent decline that is crystallized by a forced sale, a margin call, a foreclosure, or a death is a permanent loss. The same market move produces different outcomes in each case, and the difference is determined by the investor's circumstances, not by the market's behavior.

These three ideas, volatility, drawdown, and permanent loss, form the vocabulary the rest of the chapter uses. When the next section reports that the S&P 500 has a long-run annual standard deviation in the high teens and the Case-Shiller index has a long-run annual standard deviation closer to the mid-single digits, that is a comparison on volatility alone. When it reports that the S&P 500 fell roughly 56.8 percent from its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 trough, that is a comparison on drawdown. And when it asks how many of the investors who experienced that drawdown actually recovered from it, that is a question about permanent loss, and the answer depends on what those investors were forced to do in the middle of the decline.

## Market Risk: Volatility and Drawdowns

On the most straightforward measure, stocks are meaningfully more volatile than unleveraged residential real estate. Using the Damodaran dataset referenced in Chapter 1 and Chapter 5, annual total returns for the S&P 500 since 1928 have a standard deviation in the high teens, with individual-year returns ranging from roughly minus 44 percent (1931) to plus 52 percent (1954).[^1] The S&P/Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index, the standard long-run series for U.S. residential price levels, shows a much narrower dispersion, with annual nominal price changes ranging from roughly minus 18 percent (2008) to plus 18 percent (2005) and a standard deviation in the mid-single digits over the post-war period.[^2] On that comparison alone, stocks are perhaps two to three times as volatile as housing.

The drawdown comparison tells a similar story with important qualifications. The four largest post-war drawdowns for the S&P 500 on a total-return basis are the 1973 to 1974 bear market (roughly minus 48 percent), the 2000 to 2002 dot-com bear market (roughly minus 49 percent on the S&P 500 and roughly minus 78 percent on the NASDAQ Composite peak to trough), the 2007 to 2009 global financial crisis (roughly minus 56.8 percent), and the February to March 2020 COVID crash (roughly minus 33.9 percent peak to trough, the fastest bear market on record).[^1] The Case-Shiller National Index's worst post-war drawdown, by contrast, was the roughly 27 percent nominal decline from its July 2006 peak to its February 2012 trough, a much shallower decline that unfolded over six years rather than months.[^2] The absolute depth of the housing drawdown was smaller than any of the four listed stock drawdowns, and by that measure unleveraged residential real estate has been the safer asset class.

Two features of the housing number complicate this conclusion. First, the Case-Shiller index is a repeat-sales index that averages transaction prices across a large number of properties, and the averaging process smooths over the much larger price changes experienced by individual homes. A single house in a weak submarket can fall 50 percent while the national index is showing a 15 percent decline, and the individual owner of that single house experiences the 50 percent number, not the 15 percent number. Appraisal-based commercial real estate indices exhibit the same smoothing, and academic work on the difference between transaction-based and appraisal-based volatility (notably by David Geltner and colleagues at MIT) has generally concluded that the true volatility of real estate held at the individual-asset level is significantly higher than the headline index volatility suggests, though still lower than the S&P 500.

Second, and more important for the comparison, housing volatility figures are unleveraged. Once a typical 75 to 80 percent loan-to-value mortgage is applied, the volatility of the investor's equity position is amplified by a factor of roughly four to five, matching or exceeding the volatility of the unlevered S&P 500 on an apples-to-apples basis. The Chapter 5 worked example made this point on the return side: a 5 percent unlevered annual return becomes something like a 20 percent return on equity at 80 percent LTV. The same amplification applies to losses. A 20 percent decline in the underlying property becomes a total wipeout of the equity, a drawdown that no broadly diversified stock index has ever delivered in the post-war period. The right statement is therefore not that real estate is less volatile than stocks. It is that unleveraged real estate is less volatile than unleveraged stocks, and that the conventional way to hold real estate is leveraged while the conventional way to hold stocks is not.

The volatility comparison also looks different on different time scales. Daily stock prices are dramatically more volatile than daily housing prices, because there are no daily housing prices to speak of. Monthly and annual volatility narrows the gap but does not eliminate it. Over rolling five-year and ten-year windows, the two asset classes look more similar than the single-year numbers suggest, because the stock market's large negative years tend to be followed by large positive years that offset them, while the housing market's slow declines tend to persist for longer but at lower magnitudes. The investor who compares stocks and housing on the basis of daily price movements is comparing a visible asset against an invisible one, and the conclusion that stocks are riskier is partly an artifact of the difference in measurement frequency, not a difference in underlying economic risk.

## Idiosyncratic Risk and Diversification

Market-level volatility is only half of the risk story. The other half is **idiosyncratic risk** (the portion of an investment's return variability that is specific to the individual asset rather than to the market as a whole, sometimes called unsystematic or diversifiable risk). Idiosyncratic risk is what happens to one company's share price when it misses an earnings report, loses a key executive, or is sued; it is what happens to one rental property when the roof fails, the tenant stops paying, or the neighborhood declines. Market-level statistics wash idiosyncratic risk out by construction, but the individual investor holding a small number of individual assets does not get that benefit for free.

The mechanics of diversifying away idiosyncratic risk differ sharply between the two asset classes. A single purchase of a broad-market stock index fund, whether a mutual fund or an ETF, instantly delivers ownership of 500 or 3,000 or more underlying companies in proportions set by the index methodology. The expense ratio for this service is in the single-digit basis points for the largest funds, as Chapter 2 noted, and the minimum investment is the price of one share or less with fractional-share trading. A retail investor can achieve near-complete diversification of stock-market idiosyncratic risk in a single click for a total all-in cost of a fraction of a percent per year. That is a historically recent development, unavailable to the investors who lived through the crashes described in Chapter 1, and it is a defining feature of modern stock investing.

The equivalent exercise in direct real estate is an order of magnitude harder. A single property is a single property, and an investor who owns one house in one ZIP code has taken on the full idiosyncratic risk of that house and that ZIP code. Diversifying that risk requires buying more properties, which requires more capital, more transaction costs, more time, more mortgages, and more operational effort. A portfolio of ten single-family rentals spread across three metros is a meaningful diversification relative to one property, but it is a two-to-three-million-dollar commitment at 2026 U.S. median prices, and the round-trip transaction costs introduced in Chapter 3 apply on every purchase and every eventual sale. The practical consequence is that most individual real estate investors are materially less diversified than most individual stock investors, not because they choose to be but because the unit size and transaction structure of direct real estate makes diversification expensive.

Indirect real estate exposure changes this picture considerably. A single purchase of a broad REIT index fund delivers proportional ownership of roughly 150 listed U.S. equity REITs across dozens of property types and thousands of underlying properties, with expense ratios comparable to broad stock index funds. A REIT investor achieves the same kind of diversification a stock investor takes for granted, for the same kind of cost. The tradeoff, developed in later chapters, is that REIT shares behave in the short run more like stocks than like directly held real estate, and the risk profile they deliver is therefore not identical to the risk profile of a directly owned rental property. An investor who wants the diversification benefits of index-like exposure with something close to the risk profile of direct ownership does not have a clean off-the-shelf product available, and the gap is one of the reasons direct real estate and REIT investing are treated as related but not interchangeable throughout this book.

The comparison on idiosyncratic risk therefore reverses the comparison on market volatility. On market-level volatility, unleveraged stocks look riskier than unleveraged housing. On idiosyncratic risk faced by a typical individual investor, unleveraged stocks look far less risky than directly owned residential real estate, because the stock investor holds a diversified basket as a matter of routine and the real estate investor holds a concentrated single asset almost by necessity. Which of those two numbers matters more depends on how much of the portfolio sits in each asset class and on whether the real estate exposure is direct or indirect. For readers who hold one rental house and an index fund, the two comparisons point in opposite directions, and any honest risk assessment has to account for both.

## Leverage Risk Revisited

Chapter 5 introduced leverage as the arithmetic that turns an unlevered real estate return into a competitive return on invested equity. The same arithmetic, seen from the risk side, is a tail-amplification mechanism. At an 80 percent loan-to-value ratio, a 20 percent decline in the underlying property fully eliminates the investor's equity before accounting for transaction costs and delinquency. At a 90 percent loan-to-value ratio, which is available on some owner-occupied and government-backed products, a 10 percent decline does the same thing. Stocks held on margin under Regulation T face an analogous amplification but capped at a smaller multiplier, with a much more responsive collateral call mechanism on the way down.

The mechanical difference between a mortgage call and a margin call is more important than the leverage ratio alone. A stock investor whose margin account falls below the maintenance requirement faces an immediate demand for additional cash, and a failure to meet that demand results in the broker liquidating positions at current market prices, often within days or hours. The forced sale happens at the worst possible moment, exactly when the market is stressed and prices are depressed, and the loss is crystallized before any recovery can occur. This is the mechanism that turns a temporary volatility event into a permanent loss of capital for leveraged stock investors, and it is the single most important reason equity margin debt is treated in the professional literature as a category of risk distinct from the stock exposure itself.

A residential mortgage has no equivalent mechanism. A homeowner or rental investor whose property falls 40 percent in value is not required to post additional collateral. The loan documents contain no mark-to-market trigger, and as long as the borrower continues making the scheduled monthly payments, the lender has no contractual right to accelerate the loan or force a sale. The borrower who is underwater but current on payments is effectively holding a long position in the property with no call from the lender, and historical work on the 2008 crisis (notably by Ferreira, Gyourko, and Tracy and by Foote, Gerardi, and Willen) has shown that a substantial majority of underwater homeowners who continued making payments eventually recovered fully as prices rebounded in the 2010s. The borrowers who lost everything were the ones who could no longer make the payment, typically because of a job loss or other income shock, and whose properties then entered the multi-month foreclosure process described in Chapter 10.

The practical lesson is that mortgage leverage is less dangerous than equity margin leverage on a per-dollar basis when the borrower's income is stable, because there is no mark-to-market call to force a sale at the bottom. It is more dangerous than equity margin leverage when the borrower's income is not stable, because the amplification factor is larger and the illiquidity of the underlying asset makes a voluntary sale at the bottom expensive and slow. Leverage risk in real estate is therefore tightly coupled to income stability in a way that leverage risk in stocks is not. An investor with a highly stable income stream can safely take on more real estate leverage than an investor with a volatile income stream, and the same arithmetic that makes the investor's return depend on the property also makes it depend on the investor's ability to keep writing the mortgage check during a downturn. Chapter 11 develops the full leverage treatment; Chapter 6's contribution is to flag that the leverage decision is a risk decision first and a return decision second.

## Liquidity and Life-Event Risk

The final category of risk to develop before turning to tail events is **liquidity risk** (the risk that an investor will be unable to convert an asset to cash at or near its fair market value when cash is needed, forcing either a distressed sale or a delay). Liquidity risk is the mechanism by which market risk and leverage risk most commonly become permanent losses. An investor who can hold through a drawdown rarely suffers a permanent loss from it; an investor who cannot hold through a drawdown almost always does.

Stocks and residential real estate sit at opposite ends of the liquidity spectrum for individual investors. A position in a broad U.S. stock index fund can be sold on any trading day, the cash settles under the T plus one rule introduced in Chapter 2, and the total round-trip cost is effectively zero at any broker offering commission-free trading. An investor who needs cash on short notice for a medical emergency, a job loss, or a family event can convert a brokerage position to cash in hours without meaningful price concession. A rental property, by contrast, requires a listing agent, showings, offers, negotiations, inspection and appraisal contingencies, a financing contingency on the buyer's side, and a closing process that routinely takes 30 to 60 days from accepted offer even in normal markets. The round-trip transaction cost is the 7 to 11 percent flagged in Chapter 3, and in distressed markets the time on market lengthens and the negotiated price concession grows. Chapter 10 develops liquidity in full.

The risk consequence of that gap is that life events that affect both asset classes produce very different outcomes on each side. A medical bill that forces a stock investor to sell in a down market costs the paper loss but leaves the investor with most of the original capital. The same medical bill that forces a rental owner to sell in a down market can cost the paper loss plus the full transaction cost plus an additional distressed-sale concession, and in a leveraged position after a price decline the sale can wipe out the equity entirely. The stock investor's drawdown is converted to a small permanent loss; the real estate investor's drawdown is converted to a much larger one. The asset with the lower headline volatility produces the larger realized loss in this scenario, and the mechanism that does the conversion is liquidity risk, not market risk. Sarah Okonkwo's question about 2008, addressed later in this chapter, is ultimately a question about exactly this mechanism.

## Tail Risk and Black Swans

The risk categories so far, market volatility, idiosyncratic shocks, leverage, and liquidity, describe the routine ways investments lose money. **Tail risk** (the risk of rare, high-magnitude events that fall outside the range of normal historical experience and are poorly described by the assumption that returns are normally distributed) describes the way investments lose money on the rare occasions when the routine categories all compound at once. Chapter 1 walked through the major post-war tail events, 1929 to 1932, 1973 to 1974, 2000 to 2002, 2007 to 2009, and February to March 2020, and they are worth revisiting here through the risk lens rather than the return lens.

The 2007 to 2009 global financial crisis is the cleanest example because it was a tail event for both asset classes simultaneously. The S&P 500 fell roughly 56.8 percent on a total-return basis from its October 9, 2007 peak to its March 9, 2009 trough, and the Case-Shiller National Home Price Index fell roughly 27 percent nominal from July 2006 to February 2012 according to the same FRED series used in Chapter 1 and Chapter 5.[^1][^2] The routine story says that stocks lost more than real estate on a percentage basis, and on the unleveraged measure that story is correct. The tail story is that the correlation between the two asset classes, normally assumed to be modest, spiked toward one during the crisis, and investors who had relied on stock and real estate exposure to diversify each other found that both were losing money at the same time. Diversification is a concept that works well in the middle of the distribution and performs worst precisely in the tails where it is needed most, and this is a general feature of financial markets rather than a 2008-specific quirk.

For stock investors, the tail in 2008 played out as a fast, visible, widely shared drawdown that bottomed within eighteen months and recovered to its previous high by March 2013. Investors who held the index through the full drawdown suffered no permanent loss and captured the subsequent decade of gains. Investors who sold at or near the bottom, typically because the pain had become intolerable or because a margin call or life event forced the sale, crystallized the paper loss into a realized loss. The tail risk in stocks is therefore mostly a behavioral risk during the drawdown itself, not an asset-class risk, and the investor's temperament and cash reserves are the key determinants of whether the tail event damages the long-run plan.

For real estate investors, the same tail played out very differently. The national drawdown was shallower and slower, but it was concentrated in specific metros (Las Vegas, Phoenix, parts of Florida, parts of California's Inland Empire) where prices fell 50 percent or more, well outside the national index. Leverage ratios of 90 to 97 percent on owner-occupied and FHA products meant that modest declines in the underlying property produced total wipeouts of homeowner equity, which then produced foreclosures, which then produced forced sales at further-depressed prices. The feedback loop between foreclosure, forced sale, and further price decline is a mechanism the stock market does not have in the same form, because the stock market's equivalent of forced selling (margin calls) happens over hours rather than the years of the foreclosure pipeline, and because stock index constituents do not include the specific firms that the crisis damaged in the way a housing market includes the specific homes whose owners were foreclosed. The result was that the unleveraged real estate index number understated the lived experience of individual investors in the hardest-hit submarkets by a wide margin, while the leveraged equity return was far more negative than the index number alone would suggest.

The COVID crash of February to March 2020 is the counterexample. Stocks fell roughly 34 percent in just over a month, the fastest bear market on record, and then recovered within about five months.[^1] The Case-Shiller National Index did not fall at all; it began a 40 percent run that continued into mid-2022.[^2] For that episode, stocks were the asset class that experienced the visible tail event and real estate was the asset class that experienced the opposite. The generalizable point is that tail risk is not a fixed property of an asset class. It is a description of what can happen under conditions that have not yet occurred, and the two asset classes have had different tail events at different times for different reasons, with no reliable pattern as to which asset will be the one to crash next.

One additional category of tail risk is unique to direct real estate and deserves an explicit mention: **catastrophic physical loss** (the destruction or severe impairment of a specific property by fire, flood, wind, earthquake, or other physical event). Insurance is designed to absorb most of this risk and, when in force, converts a potentially total loss into a recoverable one. Uninsured or underinsured losses, including flood damage in areas outside mandatory National Flood Insurance Program zones and earthquake damage in areas without separate earthquake policies, can convert a single property into a zero. Stock investors face no comparable risk at the index level, though individual public companies can be destroyed by catastrophes; the diversification of the index absorbs those losses. Climate change is gradually widening the set of properties exposed to uninsurable or increasingly expensive insurance risk, and Chapter 16 returns to this as one of the future-facing variables that may shift the comparison between stocks and direct real estate over the next several decades.

## Persona: Sarah Okonkwo and What 2008 Actually Taught Her

Sarah Okonkwo is 45 years old and a registered nurse and hospital shift supervisor in Atlanta. Her household income is about $98,000, her retirement accounts hold approximately $180,000, she has $45,000 in liquid savings, and she owns her primary residence, which she bought in 2015 with $142,000 still owed on the mortgage. She has a small taxable brokerage account of about $15,000 and no rental properties. Her time available for active investment management is very limited because she works rotating shifts, and her self-reported risk tolerance is moderate-low. The question that brings her to this chapter is the one quoted at the beginning: "I lived through 2008 and it scared me. What's actually safer, more real estate or more stocks?"

Sarah's question is usually asked as if the answer is a single number. It is not. What 2008 actually taught her, once the risk categories in this chapter are laid out, is that the two asset classes have different risk profiles in different dimensions, and the right asset for her depends on which of those dimensions she is most exposed to. On market volatility, stocks are the scarier chart, and Sarah's memory of the 2008 to 2009 drawdown is built around the visible stock decline she saw on the evening news. On idiosyncratic and concentration risk, direct real estate is the scarier exposure, especially for someone with only enough capital for one additional property rather than a diversified portfolio of several. On leverage risk, a second mortgaged property is far more dangerous to Sarah's financial stability than an unleveraged stock position of the same dollar size, because her income is exposed to the same kind of recession that would produce the property price decline, and because her shifts are not guaranteed in a severe downturn. On liquidity and life-event risk, the rental property is the asset that would hurt her most if she needed to sell at a bad moment, and a nurse working rotating shifts has a realistic probability of needing to manage an unplanned family or health event.

The honest answer for Sarah is that "more stocks" and "more real estate" are not symmetric options. More stocks, in her situation, means incrementally more exposure to the visible market volatility she already fears but that has historically mean-reverted over the horizons that matter to her. More real estate, in her situation, means a concentrated, leveraged, illiquid position in a single property whose risk is tightly coupled to her own job stability and to a local market she cannot diversify out of. The stock option scares her more on the surface and is probably less dangerous to her actual financial plan. The real estate option feels more solid and is probably more dangerous, because its risks are hidden in the dimensions she does not instinctively track. Chapters 7 through 12 equip Sarah with the additional lenses she needs to make the call, and Chapter 17 provides the scoring model that translates those lenses into a recommendation. Chapter 6's contribution is simply to make clear that "What's actually safer?" is not a question about a single volatility number. It is a question about which category of risk matters most for her specific situation, and the answer is usually not the one that the evening news made most memorable.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Volatility, drawdown, and permanent loss of capital are three distinct ideas, not one.** Standard deviation describes how bumpy the ride is; drawdown describes how deep the worst decline is; permanent loss describes how much of that decline is never recovered. Most of the work of risk management is in the gap between the second and the third.

2. **On pure market volatility, unleveraged stocks are meaningfully more volatile than unleveraged residential real estate, by a factor of roughly two to three.** The S&P 500 has an annual standard deviation in the high teens since 1928; the Case-Shiller National Index has one in the mid-single digits over the post-war period.

3. **The index-level housing volatility figure understates individual-property volatility and ignores leverage.** Repeat-sales indices smooth across properties, and a typical 75 to 80 percent LTV mortgage amplifies equity volatility by a factor of four to five. Levered real estate is not obviously less volatile than unlevered stocks.

4. **Idiosyncratic risk flips the comparison.** A single stock-index-fund purchase delivers instant diversification across hundreds of companies at near-zero cost. A single rental property delivers the opposite: full exposure to one house in one ZIP code. For most individual investors, directly held real estate is far more concentrated than their stock holdings.

5. **Mortgage leverage and equity margin leverage are not the same kind of risk.** Margin debt calls mark-to-market and forces sales at the bottom; mortgage debt does not as long as payments continue. Mortgage leverage is therefore more tightly coupled to the borrower's income stability than to asset price alone, and a job loss in a downturn is the real trigger for permanent loss in leveraged real estate.

6. **Liquidity risk is the mechanism that most often converts drawdowns into permanent losses.** Stocks can be converted to cash in hours at near-zero cost; real estate takes weeks to months and 7 to 11 percent round-trip. Life events that arrive during a drawdown damage real estate positions far more than stock positions of the same dollar size.

7. **Tail risk is not a fixed property of an asset class.** 2008 was a tail event for both stocks and real estate, but most severely for leveraged real estate in specific metros. COVID was a tail event for stocks with no corresponding housing decline. The next tail event will look like neither, and planning for risk means planning for categories of shock rather than for a specific historical replay.

8. **"Which is safer?" is the wrong question if it is asked as a single number.** The right question is which dimensions of risk the investor is most exposed to given their income stability, time horizon, liquidity needs, and concentration. For readers like Sarah Okonkwo, the asset class with the scary headlines is often the one with the smaller risk to her actual financial plan, and the asset class that feels solid is often the one with the more damaging failure mode.
